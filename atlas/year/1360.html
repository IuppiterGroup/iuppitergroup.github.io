    The chronicle of these years

    • The Black Death ArrivesA.D. 1347, Messina, Sicily. In October 1347 Genoese galleys from the Black Sea put in at Messina with dying men at the oars. The plague they carried, borne along the trade routes by rats and fleas, swept Italy, France, England, and Germany within three years and killed perhaps a third or more of Europe's people. Labor grew dear, serfdom loosened, and the faith and art of Europe were shadowed for generations.
    • The Red Turbans RiseA.D. 1351, Yingzhou, Anhui, China. The Yuan conscripted 150,000 labourers to repair the broken dikes of the Yellow River, and among them the White Lotus sect of Han Shantong spread the word that a one-eyed stone man would be dug up to herald the fall of the dynasty. The Red Turban armies rose along the Huai, and within a decade the Yuan had lost the south to rebel warlords.
    • U Thong Founds AyutthayaA.D. 1351, Ayutthaya, Thailand. Prince U Thong, fleeing, the chronicles say, from an epidemic, crossed to an island where three rivers meet and there founded Ayutthaya, taking the name Ramathibodi. Heir to the Khmer arts of government and the Mon and Thai traditions of the Chao Phraya basin, his city ruled Siam for four centuries and became one of the richest ports of Asia.
    • Fa Ngum Founds Lan XangA.D. 1353, Xieng Thong, Laos. Fa Ngum, a Lao prince raised in exile at the Khmer court of Angkor, marched up the Mekong with a Khmer army and his wife, a Cambodian princess, and was crowned at Xieng Dong Xieng Thong. He named his realm Lan Xang, the Million Elephants, and Khmer monks brought with them the Theravada faith and, by tradition, the golden image called the Phra Bang.
    • The Ottomans Cross to EuropeA.D. 1354, Gallipoli, Thrace. Orhan's son Süleyman had led Turks into Thrace as allies of John VI Kantakouzenos in his civil war. When an earthquake shook down the walls of Gallipoli on the Dardanelles in March 1354, Süleyman's men occupied the ruins and refused to leave. Settled with Turkish colonists, it was the first lasting Ottoman foothold in Europe, from which they spread across Thrace.
    • The Golden Bull of Charles IVA.D. 1356, Nuremberg, Germany. At diets in Nuremberg and Metz, Charles IV issued a law sealed in gold that fixed the election of the King of the Romans in seven electors: the archbishops of Mainz, Cologne, and Trier, the King of Bohemia, the Count Palatine, the Duke of Saxony, and the Margrave of Brandenburg. The pope's approval went unmentioned. The bull governed the Empire until 1806.
    • Zhu Yuanzhang Founds the MingA.D. 1368, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China. Zhu Yuanzhang, an orphan of famine who had begged as a monk and risen through the Red Turban armies, proclaimed the Ming, the Brilliant dynasty, at Nanjing. That summer his general Xu Da marched north and took Dadu without a battle, and the last Yuan emperor, Toghon Temür, fled to the steppe. China was ruled by a Chinese house once more.
    • Timur Master of TransoxianaA.D. 1370, Balkh, Khorasan. Timur, a petty noble of the Barlas tribe lamed by arrows in his youth, besieged his former ally and brother-in-law Amir Husayn in Balkh, and on Husayn's death in April 1370 was proclaimed sovereign of the Chagatai lands. Not being of Genghis Khan's blood, he ruled in the name of puppet khans, taking only the styles of emir and royal son-in-law.
