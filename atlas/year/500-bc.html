    The chronicle of these years

    • Rome Expels Its Last King509 B.C., Forum, Rome. After the rape of the virtuous Lucretia by the king's son, Lucius Junius Brutus led the Romans in driving out Tarquin the Proud and all his house. They swore never again to suffer a king, and in his place elected two consuls for a year at a time. In the same year, tradition says, the temple of Jupiter on the Capitol was dedicated.
    • Cleisthenes Gives Athens Democracy508 B.C., Athens, Greece. When the tyrant's family had been expelled and the Spartan king Cleomenes tried to set up an oligarchy, the Athenian people rose and recalled Cleisthenes. He broke the power of the old clans by dividing the citizens into ten new tribes drawn from the city, the coast, and the inland, and founded the council of five hundred: the government of the people, demokratia, was born.
    • The Bronze Drums of Dong Sonc. 500 B.C., Dong Son, Thanh Hoa, Vietnam. On the Ma River the lords of the Dong Son culture cast great bronze drums, their faces ringed with flying herons, feathered warriors, and long boats about a central star. Traded and treasured from Yunnan to the Moluccas, the drums are the emblem of the first rice-growing chiefdoms of the Red River country, which the Vietnamese remember as the realm of the Hung kings.
    • The Terracottas of Nokc. 500 B.C., Nok, Nigeria. On the Jos Plateau a people known to us only by the village where tin miners first turned up their work modeled life-sized heads in fired clay, with pierced eyes and elaborate coiffures. At sites such as Taruga they were smelting iron by the fourth century before Christ, among the earliest ironworkers of Africa south of the Sahara.
    • The Zapotecs Found Monte Albánc. 500 B.C., Monte Albán, Mexico. The Zapotec lords of the Valley of Oaxaca leveled the summit of a mountain four hundred metres above the valley floor and built there a capital of plazas, temples, and ballcourts that endured for more than a thousand years. On the slabs of its gallery of Danzantes they carved hundreds of slain or captive enemies, beside some of the earliest hieroglyphic writing of the Americas.
    • The Athenians at Marathon490 B.C., Marathon, Greece. Darius sent Datis and Artaphernes across the Aegean to punish Athens. On the plain of Marathon some 10,000 Athenians and 1,000 Plataeans under Miltiades charged at a run, broke the Persian wings, and drove the invaders to their ships, losing 192 men against 6,400 Persians. The runner Pheidippides had gone to Sparta for help, which came too late.
    • Thermopylae and Salamis480 B.C., Thermopylae, Greece. Leonidas and three hundred Spartans, with their Greek allies, hold the pass at Thermopylae against the host of Xerxes for three days. Betrayed by a mountain path, the Spartans fight to the death. Weeks later the Athenian fleet destroys the Persian navy in the strait of Salamis, and Greece is saved.
    • The Death of Confucius479 B.C., Qufu, Shandong, China. Kong Qiu, Master Kong, died in Lu at seventy-three, never having found a prince who would put his teaching into practice. He taught that order rested on ritual, filial duty, and the cultivation of humane virtue in rulers and ministers alike. His disciples set down his sayings in the Analects, and his teaching became the moral foundation of China, Korea, Vietnam, and Japan.
