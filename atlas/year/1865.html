    The chronicle of these years

    • The Emancipation ProclamationA.D. 1863, Washington, D.C.. Taking the occasion of the Union's narrow victory at Antietam, Lincoln proclaimed that on 1 January 1863 all slaves in the states in rebellion would be 'then, thenceforward, and forever free.' The proclamation made the war a war against slavery, opened the Union armies to black soldiers, of whom some 180,000 served, and led to the Thirteenth Amendment of 1865.
    • GettysburgA.D. 1863, Gettysburg, Pennsylvania. Robert E. Lee's second invasion of the North met George Meade's Army of the Potomac at Gettysburg in the first three days of July 1863. After two days of savage fighting at Little Round Top and Culp's Hill, Pickett's charge against the Union center failed; the two armies lost some fifty thousand men killed, wounded, or captured, and Lee never again invaded the North. The next day Vicksburg surrendered to Grant.
    • The Circassian ExodusA.D. 1864, Sochi, Circassia. Russia's long war for the western Caucasus ended in May 1864 with a victory parade at Kbaada above Sochi. The Circassian tribes who would not move to the plains were driven to the Black Sea shore and shipped to Ottoman lands; hundreds of thousands were expelled, and tens of thousands died of hunger and typhus in the ports and on the voyage. Their descendants live from Turkey to Jordan.
    • The Fall of the Heavenly CapitalA.D. 1864, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China. After a siege of two years the Hunan Army of Zeng Guofan, led by his brother Zeng Guoquan, blew a breach in the walls of Nanjing and stormed the Taiping capital. Hong Xiuquan had died weeks before, and his followers were slaughtered in their tens of thousands. The provincial armies that had saved the dynasty became the power behind it.
    • Lee Surrenders at AppomattoxA.D. 1865, Appomattox Court House, Virginia. With Richmond fallen and his starving army cut off in its retreat west, Robert E. Lee surrendered to Ulysses S. Grant in the parlor of the McLean house at Appomattox Court House on 9 April 1865. The war that had cost some 620,000 lives preserved the Union and ended slavery in the United States; five days later Lincoln was shot by John Wilkes Booth at Ford's Theatre.
    • KöniggrätzA.D. 1866, Königgrätz, Bohemia. On 3 July 1866 three Prussian armies, moved by railway and directed by telegraph by General von Moltke, converged on Benedek's Austrian army at Königgrätz in Bohemia; the Crown Prince's arrival on the Austrian flank in the afternoon decided the day. The war lasted seven weeks. Austria was shut out of Germany, and Prussia annexed Hanover, Hesse-Kassel, Nassau, and Frankfurt.
    • The Meiji RestorationA.D. 1868, Heian-kyō, Kyoto, Japan. Young samurai of Satsuma and Chōshū, resolved that Japan should not share China's humiliation, forced the last shogun, Tokugawa Yoshinobu, to give up his power, and proclaimed the rule of the boy Emperor Meiji. Within a generation they abolished the domains and the samurai class, built railways, factories, and a conscript army, and made Japan the first industrial power of Asia.
    • The Suez Canal OpensA.D. 1869, Port Said, Egypt. Ferdinand de Lesseps had begun digging at Port Said in 1859 under a concession from the viceroy Said, and tens of thousands of Egyptian peasants were drafted to the work. On 17 November 1869, before the Empress Eugénie and the princes of Europe, the khedive Isma'il opened the canal from the Mediterranean to the Red Sea. The voyage from London to Bombay was shortened by some four thousand miles.
