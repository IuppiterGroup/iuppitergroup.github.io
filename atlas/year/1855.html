    The chronicle of these years

    • The Coup of the Second of DecemberA.D. 1851, Paris, France. Barred by the constitution from a second term, President Louis-Napoléon Bonaparte struck on 2 December 1851, the anniversary of Austerlitz: troops occupied Paris, the leading deputies were arrested, and resistance on the boulevards was shot down; Victor Hugo, who had tried to raise the barricades, fled into exile. A plebiscite approved the coup, and a year later to the day he was proclaimed Napoleon III.
    • Perry's Black ShipsA.D. 1853, Uraga, Kanagawa, Japan. Commodore Matthew Perry steamed into Edo Bay with four black ships, their guns run out, and delivered a letter from President Fillmore asking that Japan open its ports. He returned the next year with a larger squadron, and the shogunate signed the Treaty of Kanagawa. Two centuries of seclusion were over, and the authority of the shogun began to crumble.
    • The First Train in IndiaA.D. 1853, Mumbai, Maharashtra, India. On 16 April 1853 a train of fourteen carriages drawn by three locomotives, Sultan, Sindh, and Sahib, ran thirty-four kilometres from Bori Bunder in Bombay to Thane with four hundred guests aboard, to a salute of twenty-one guns. Within half a century India had one of the largest railway systems on earth, binding the subcontinent together.
    • The Bowring TreatyA.D. 1855, Bangkok, Siam. King Mongkut, a monk for twenty-seven years who read Latin and English, chose to bend before the West rather than break. With Sir John Bowring, governor of Hong Kong, he signed a treaty opening Siam to free trade at a duty of three percent and granting British subjects their own courts. Like treaties with other powers followed, and Siam kept its independence.
    • The Rising at MeerutA.D. 1857, Meerut, Uttar Pradesh, India. On 10 May 1857, after eighty-five troopers had been fettered for refusing cartridges rumoured to be greased with cow and pig fat, the sepoys at Meerut rose, shot their officers, and rode for Delhi. There they proclaimed the aged Bahadur Shah II emperor of Hindustan. Rebellion spread across the north, and the British needed more than a year to put it down.
    • The Massacre at CawnporeA.D. 1857, Kanpur, Uttar Pradesh, India. Nana Sahib, adopted son of the last Peshwa, besieged the British entrenchment at Cawnpore for three weeks and then offered safe passage downriver. At the Satichaura ghat the boats were fired upon and most of the men killed. The surviving women and children, some two hundred, were held in the Bibighar and hacked to death as a relieving column drew near. British vengeance was merciless.
    • Mindon Founds MandalayA.D. 1857, Mandalay, Burma. King Mindon, who had seized the throne after the British took Lower Burma in 1852, founded a new royal city at the foot of Mandalay Hill, where the Buddha was said to have foretold a great city. A devout and gentle ruler, he held the Fifth Buddhist Council there in 1871 and had the whole Pali canon carved on 729 marble slabs at the Kuthodaw pagoda.
    • The Crown Takes IndiaA.D. 1858, Prayagraj, Uttar Pradesh, India. The Government of India Act ended the rule of the East India Company, and on 1 November 1858 Lord Canning read at Allahabad Queen Victoria's proclamation promising religious toleration and equal law to her Indian subjects. Bahadur Shah II, the last Mughal emperor, was tried and exiled to Rangoon, and the British Raj began.
