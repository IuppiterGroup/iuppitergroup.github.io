    The chronicle of these years

    • Kublai Proclaims the YuanA.D. 1271, Khanbaliq, China. Kublai, who had won a four-year succession war against his brother Ariq Böke, named his dynasty Yuan, the Origin, from the Book of Changes, and was raising a new capital, Dadu, the Khanbaliq of the Turks and of Marco Polo, beside the ruins of Zhongdu. He ruled China as emperor and the Mongols as Great Khan, though the western khanates went their own way.
    • Rudolf of Habsburg on the MarchfeldA.D. 1278, Dürnkrut, Austria. Ottokar II of Bohemia, the iron and golden king whose lands stretched from the Bohemian forests to the Adriatic, refused to yield Austria to the new German king, Rudolf of Habsburg. On 26 August 1278 at Dürnkrut on the Marchfeld, Rudolf's reserve, held back against the customs of chivalry, struck the Bohemian flank, and Ottokar was slain. Austria was the Habsburgs' for 640 years.
    • The Battle of YamenA.D. 1279, Yamen, Guangdong, China. The last Song loyalists made their stand with a great fleet in the bay of Yamen near Canton. When the Yuan admiral Zhang Hongfan broke their lines, the minister Lu Xiufu took the boy emperor Zhao Bing on his back and leapt into the sea. The Song dynasty perished, and all China passed for the first time under a conquering people of the steppe.
    • Polynesians Settle Aotearoac. A.D. 1280, Wairau Bar, New Zealand. Voyaging canoes from eastern Polynesia, remembered in Maori tradition by names such as Aotea, Tainui, and Te Arawa, reached the last great land on earth to be settled by man. At Wairau Bar the first settlers buried their dead with drilled moa eggs and necklaces of moa-bone reels; within two centuries the giant moa were hunted to extinction, and the newcomers had become the Maori.
    • The Divine WindA.D. 1281, Takashima, Nagasaki, Japan. Seven years after a first landing at Hakata had been driven off, Kublai sent two fleets, said to carry 140,000 men, against Japan. The samurai held the stone wall built along Hakata Bay, and in the summer a typhoon wrecked the Yuan ships gathered off Takashima. The Japanese called it the kamikaze, the divine wind, and the invasion was never tried again.
    • The Sicilian VespersA.D. 1282, Palermo, Sicily. At the hour of vespers on Easter Monday 1282, outside the church of the Holy Spirit at Palermo, a French soldier insulted a Sicilian wife, and the crowd killed him. The rising spread through the island, and some thousands of Frenchmen were massacred. The Sicilians offered their crown to Peter III of Aragon, and Charles of Anjou lost the island forever.
    • The Fall of PaganA.D. 1287, Pagan, Burma. King Narathihapate, who had refused tribute to Kublai Khan, fled south before the Mongols, earning the name Tayokpyemin, the king who ran from the Chinese, and was poisoned by his son at Prome. A Yuan army pressed down the Irrawaddy, and the empire of Anawrahta broke apart among Mon kings at Martaban, Shan lords in the hills, and three brothers in the Kyaukse valley.
    • Tran Hung Dao at the Bach DangA.D. 1288, Bach Dang River, Vietnam. In the third Mongol invasion the Tran prince Tran Hung Dao repeated the stratagem of Ngo Quyen: he drove stakes into the Bach Dang, drew the Yuan fleet in on the high tide, and fell on it as the ebb impaled the ships. Four hundred vessels were taken with the admiral Omar, and Kublai's son Toghon fled north in disgrace.
