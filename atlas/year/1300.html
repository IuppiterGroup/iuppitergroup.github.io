    The chronicle of these years

    • Raden Wijaya Founds MajapahitA.D. 1293, Trowulan, Java. Kublai Khan sent a fleet to punish Kertanegara, but found him dead, slain by the rebel Jayakatwang of Kediri. Kertanegara's son-in-law Raden Wijaya joined the Mongols to destroy Jayakatwang, then turned on his allies and drove them to their ships. At the village of Majapahit, named for a bitter fruit, he founded the greatest of the Javanese empires.
    • Ghazan Khan Turns to IslamA.D. 1295, Tabriz, Persia. Ghazan, great-grandson of Hulagu, professed Islam in June 1295 to win the support of the emir Nawruz and the Muslim Mongols, and that autumn he seized the throne of the Ilkhanate. At his accession Nawruz had the churches, synagogues, and Buddhist temples of Tabriz pulled down. With his vizier Rashid al-Din, Ghazan reformed the taxes, and the Mongols of Persia became Muslims in faith.
    • Mangrai Founds Chiang MaiA.D. 1296, Chiang Mai, Thailand. Mangrai, Tai king of Ngoen Yang, having taken the old Mon city of Haripunjaya by stratagem, laid out a new capital on the Ping River, consulting, the chronicle says, with his allies Ram Khamhaeng of Sukhothai and Ngam Mueang of Phayao. Chiang Mai, the New City, became the seat of Lan Na, the kingdom of a million rice fields.
    • The Tomb of Sultan Malik al-SalehA.D. 1297, Samudra Pasai, Sumatra. At Samudra on the north coast of Sumatra died Malik al-Saleh, first Muslim ruler of Pasai, whose gravestone of 1297 is the oldest dated record of a Muslim kingdom in Southeast Asia. Marco Polo, passing in 1292, had found the people of nearby Perlak converted by Saracen merchants; from these ports of pepper and gold Islam would spread along the trade routes of the archipelago.
    • The Walls of Great Zimbabwec. A.D. 1300, Great Zimbabwe, Zimbabwe. On a granite hill near the Mutirikwi River, the Shona kings of the Zimbabwe plateau raised enclosures of fitted stone without mortar; the outer wall of the Great Enclosure runs some 250 metres and rises to eleven. From here they taxed the gold of the plateau and traded it through Sofala and Kilwa for Persian bowls and Chinese porcelain, ruling a town of perhaps ten thousand or more.
    • The Bronze Heads of Ifec. A.D. 1300, Ile-Ife, Nigeria. In Ife, holy city of the Yoruba, where Oduduwa was said to have let down the first earth upon the waters, court artists cast heads of copper and brass of a serene and lifelike beauty, probably portraits of the onis and their court. The first of them, unearthed in 1910, astonished Europe; Ife remained the sacred source from which the Yoruba kings drew their crowns.
    • Osman Defeats the Byzantines at BapheusA.D. 1302, Bapheus, near Nicomedia. Osman, son of Ertuğrul, a Turkmen chief whose frontier lands lay on the Sakarya facing Byzantium, met and routed a Byzantine force under the general Mouzalon at Bapheus near Nicomedia in July 1302. The victory brought warriors flocking to his banner from all Anatolia. From his name came that of the Ottomans, who would rule for six centuries.
    • Alauddin Storms ChittorA.D. 1303, Chittorgarh, Rajasthan, India. After a siege of some eight months Alauddin Khalji took the great Rajput fortress of Chittor from Ratnasimha of Mewar. His court poet Amir Khusrau, who was present, wrote that thirty thousand Hindus were put to the sword in a single day. The tale that the sultan coveted Ratnasimha's queen Padmini comes from a Sufi poem written by Malik Muhammad Jayasi in 1540.
