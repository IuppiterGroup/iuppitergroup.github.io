    The chronicle of these years

    • Tariq Crosses into SpainA.D. 711, Gibraltar, Spain. Tariq ibn Ziyad, a Berber freedman in the service of the governor Musa ibn Nusayr, crossed the strait with some seven thousand men in April 711 and landed beneath the rock that bears his name, Jabal Tariq. In July he destroyed the army of the Visigothic king Roderic by the Guadalete, and Roderic was never seen again. Toledo fell within months, and nearly all Spain within seven years.
    • Qutayba Takes SamarkandA.D. 712, Samarkand, Sogdia. Qutayba ibn Muslim, Umayyad governor of Khorasan, had already reduced Bukhara when in 712 he forced the surrender of Samarkand, the richest city of Sogdia. Its prince Ghurak agreed to tribute, the idols of its temples were burned, and a mosque was built and garrisoned. Within a generation the Sogdian merchant cities of the Silk Road had passed into the world of Islam.
    • Muhammad bin Qasim Conquers SindA.D. 712, Debal (Banbhore), Sindh, Pakistan. Sent by the governor al-Hajjaj, the young Arab general Muhammad bin Qasim brought siege engines by sea and stormed the port of Debal, where his troops slew its people for three days. Marching up the Indus, he killed Raja Dahir in battle near Aror in 712, took Brahmanabad and Multan, and sent Dahir's head, a fifth of the plunder, and thousands of captives to Iraq.
    • The Great Arab Siege of ConstantinopleA.D. 717, Constantinople, Thrace. In August 717 Maslama, brother of the Caliph Sulayman, invested Constantinople with a vast army while a great fleet sought to close the straits. Leo III, emperor for barely five months, burned the ships with Greek fire. A winter of deep snow, famine, and Bulgar attacks wasted the besiegers, and in August 718 the remnant withdrew. Europe's eastern gate had held.
    • Odo of Aquitaine Saves ToulouseA.D. 721, Toulouse, France. Al-Samh, governor of al-Andalus, crossed the Pyrenees, took Narbonne, and laid siege to Toulouse. After three months Duke Odo of Aquitaine fell upon the besiegers on 9 June 721 and broke them; al-Samh died of his wounds. It was the first great check to the Muslim advance beyond the Pyrenees, eleven years before Charles Martel's victory near Tours.
    • Pelayo at Covadongac. A.D. 722, Covadonga, Asturias. Below a cave shrine of the Virgin in the Asturian mountains, Pelayo, a Visigothic noble, and a band of followers turned on a Muslim column sent to crush them and drove it back down the valley. The fight, magnified by the chroniclers into a miracle, gave birth to the kingdom of Asturias, and Spaniards would reckon the Reconquista from that day.
    • Leo III Against the IconsA.D. 726, Constantinople, Thrace. After a volcanic eruption in the Aegean, which he took for a sign of divine anger, Leo III by the traditional account had the image of Christ taken down from the Chalke Gate of the palace, and the women of the city killed the officer who did it. So began more than a century of Iconoclasm, which set emperors against monks and popes and divided the empire to its roots.
    • ToursA.D. 732, Between Tours and Poitiers. Between Tours and Poitiers, the Frankish infantry of Charles Martel stands like a wall of ice against the cavalry of the Umayyad governor Abd al-Rahman. Abd al-Rahman is killed and his army slips away in the night. The tide of the Arab advance into Gaul has turned, and Charles is remembered as Martel, the Hammer.
