    The chronicle of these years

    • Al-Khwarizmi's Algebrac. A.D. 820, Baghdad, Iraq. In the House of Wisdom of the caliph al-Ma'mun, where the works of Aristotle, Galen, and Ptolemy were turned into Arabic, Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi wrote his book on restoring and balancing, al-jabr, from which algebra takes its name. His treatise on the Indian numerals carried the decimal place system to the West, and his own name survives in the word algorithm.
    • Borobudur Rises on the Kedu Plainc. A.D. 825, Borobudur, Java. Under the Sailendra king Samaratungga the builders finished Borobudur, a stepped mountain of some two million blocks of andesite wrapped about a hill. Pilgrims climbed its nine terraces past 2,672 relief panels telling the life of the Buddha and the quest of the youth Sudhana, to 72 latticed stupas below the summit: the greatest Buddhist monument in the world.
    • The Arabs Land in SicilyA.D. 827, Mazara, Sicily. Invited by the rebel admiral Euphemius, an Aghlabid army of some ten thousand foot and seven hundred horse under the aged jurist Asad ibn al-Furat crossed from Africa and landed at Mazara in June 827. The conquest of the island took three-quarters of a century of sieges, massacres, and enslavements; Syracuse fell in 878 and Taormina in 902.
    • Nanzhao Sacks the PyuA.D. 832, Halin, Burma. The Pyu, a Buddhist people whose walled cities of brick had ruled the dry zone of the Irrawaddy for centuries, fell prey to Nanzhao in Yunnan. In 832 its armies sacked a Pyu capital and drove three thousand captives north to the shores of the lake of Kunming. From the wreck of the Pyu rose the Burmans, who soon built their own city at Pagan.
    • The Kyrgyz Destroy the Uyghur KhaganateA.D. 840, Ordu-Baliq, Mongolia. In a year of famine, plague, and heavy snows, a rebel Uyghur minister opened the steppe to the Yenisei Kyrgyz, who stormed Ordu-Baliq, killed the qaghan, and burned the city. The Uyghurs scattered: some fled to the Tang frontier, others to the oases of Turfan and Gansu, where they founded kingdoms that lasted for centuries.
    • The Death of LangdarmaA.D. 842, Lhasa, Tibet. Langdarma, remembered in Buddhist history as a persecutor of the faith, was shot dead with an arrow by the monk Lhalung Palgyi Dorje, who by tradition escaped on a horse blackened with soot that the river washed white. The succession was disputed between two infant princes, the empire broke apart, and Tibet entered a long age of fragmentation.
    • The Treaty of VerdunA.D. 843, Verdun, Lotharingia. After three years of civil war, the bloody field of Fontenoy, and the Oaths of Strasbourg, the three surviving sons of Louis the Pious divided Charlemagne's empire. Lothair kept the imperial title and a middle kingdom from Frisia to Italy; Louis the German took the lands east of the Rhine, Charles the Bald the west. The lines drawn at Verdun foreshadowed France and Germany.
    • Kenneth MacAlpin, King of the Pictsc. A.D. 843, Forteviot, Scotland. Kenneth son of Alpin, king of the Scots of Dál Riata, made himself master of the Picts, whose nobility the Vikings had lately slaughtered in battle. From his seat at Forteviot he ruled both peoples; within two generations the Picts and their tongue had passed from history, and the kingdom of Alba, the root of Scotland, had taken their place.
