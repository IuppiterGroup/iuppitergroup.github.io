    The chronicle of these years

    • Disaster Before Syracuse413 B.C., Syracuse, Sicily. The great armada that Athens sent against Syracuse in 415 was trapped in the Great Harbor after the Spartan Gylippus came to the city's aid. Its fleet broken, the army tried to escape inland, and Demosthenes and then Nicias surrendered on the march. The survivors were penned in the stone quarries, where many died; some, it is said, were freed for reciting Euripides.
    • Lysander at Aegospotami405 B.C., Aegospotami, Thrace. The Spartan admiral Lysander, paid with Persian gold, caught the Athenian fleet drawn up on the beach at Aegospotami in the Hellespont while its crews were scattered for food, and took some 170 ships. Cut off from the grain of the Black Sea, Athens surrendered in 404, pulled down its Long Walls to the music of flutes, and submitted to the Thirty Tyrants.
    • Panini Fixes the Sanskrit Tonguec. 400 B.C., Gandhara, Pakistan. Panini of Gandhara compressed the whole grammar of Sanskrit into nearly four thousand terse rules, the Ashtadhyayi. With its roots, suffixes, and laws of sound ordered like an algebra, it fixed the classical language for two thousand years and stands as the most complete grammar of any tongue made before modern times. Later Indian learning everywhere bore its stamp.
    • The Death of Socrates399 B.C., Agora, Athens. Socrates, the philosopher who had spent his life questioning the Athenians in the marketplace, was accused of impiety and of corrupting the young. A jury of 501 citizens condemned him, and he refused to flee, drinking the hemlock among his friends. His disciple Plato made his last days the subject of the Apology, the Crito, and the Phaedo.
    • Camillus Takes Veii396 B.C., Veii, Etruria. After a siege that legend stretched to ten years like that of Troy, the dictator Marcus Furius Camillus took Veii, the great Etruscan city twelve miles from Rome, by a tunnel driven under its citadel, the story went. Its land doubled the territory of Rome, and its goddess Juno was invited to a new temple on the Aventine.
    • The Gauls Sack Romec. 390 B.C., Rome, Italy. The Senones under Brennus routed the Roman army at the river Allia and entered an undefended Rome. The Capitol held out, saved, the story went, by the sacred geese of Juno, whose cackling woke Marcus Manlius against a night assault. When the Romans haggled over a ransom of a thousand pounds of gold, Brennus threw his sword on the scales and cried, 'Woe to the vanquished.'
