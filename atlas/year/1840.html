    The chronicle of these years

    • Blood RiverA.D. 1838, Ncome River, South Africa. After Dingane had the Voortrekker leader Piet Retief and his party slain at uMgungundlovu in February, some 470 Boers under Andries Pretorius drew sixty-four wagons into a laager beside the Ncome River. On 16 December more than ten thousand Zulu warriors hurled themselves upon it; some three thousand fell, while not one Boer was killed. The trekkers kept the day ever after as a covenant with God.
    • The Rose Chamber EdictA.D. 1839, Gülhane, Constantinople. On 3 November 1839, in the rose garden of the Topkapı Palace, the foreign minister Mustafa Reşid Pasha read before the ambassadors of Europe the decree of the young Sultan Abdulmejid promising security of life, honor, and property to all subjects, Muslim and non-Muslim alike, with fair taxation and regular conscription. It opened the era of reforms called the Tanzimat.
    • Lin Zexu Destroys the OpiumA.D. 1839, Humen, Guangdong, China. Sent to Canton by the Daoguang Emperor to stamp out the opium trade, the commissioner Lin Zexu blockaded the foreign factories until the British merchants surrendered over twenty thousand chests of the drug. At Humen his men dissolved it in trenches of lime and salt water and flushed it into the sea. Britain answered with gunboats, and the First Opium War began.
    • The Treaty of WaitangiA.D. 1840, Waitangi, New Zealand. On 6 February 1840 Captain William Hobson and some forty chiefs signed a treaty on the lawn at Waitangi, later carried about the islands until more than five hundred had set their names to it. In the English text the chiefs ceded sovereignty to Queen Victoria; in the Maori, they granted governance while keeping their chieftainship, a difference that has shaped New Zealand ever since.
    • Sayyid Said Moves to ZanzibarA.D. 1840, Zanzibar, Tanzania. Sayyid Said, sultan of Oman, who had broken the Mazrui of Mombasa and won back the Swahili coast, moved his court from Muscat to Zanzibar, where he had planted the island with cloves worked by slaves. Zanzibar became the chief market of the East African trade in slaves and ivory, selling many thousands a year to the plantations, Arabia, and the Gulf, and Arab and Swahili caravans pushed inland to the Great Lakes and the Congo. The market was closed in 1873, when Britain pressed the treaty on Sultan Barghash under threat of blockade.
    • James Brooke, Rajah of SarawakA.D. 1841, Kuching, Sarawak. James Brooke, an English adventurer in his own armed schooner, helped the Brunei prince Pengiran Muda Hashim put down a revolt of Malays and Dayaks in Sarawak and was rewarded with its government. Proclaimed rajah in 1841, he hunted down Iban and Malay pirates with the Royal Navy, and his family, the White Rajahs, ruled Sarawak for a hundred years.
    • The Treaty of NankingA.D. 1842, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China. With British warships beneath the walls of Nanjing after the fall of Zhenjiang had cut the Grand Canal, the Qing signed the Treaty of Nanking aboard HMS Cornwallis. China ceded Hong Kong Island, opened five ports to trade, abolished the Canton merchant monopoly, and paid an indemnity of twenty-one million dollars.
    • The Retreat from KabulA.D. 1842, Gandamak, Afghanistan. The British army that had set Shah Shuja on the throne of Kabul was besieged in its cantonments by the Afghan chiefs. In January 1842 some 4,500 soldiers and 12,000 camp followers set out for Jalalabad through snow-choked passes under constant fire. The last stand was made at Gandamak, and of the Europeans only Dr. William Brydon rode into Jalalabad, on a wounded pony.
