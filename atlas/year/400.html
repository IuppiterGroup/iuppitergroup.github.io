    The chronicle of these years

    • The Battle of the FrigidusA.D. 394, Frigidus River, Slovenia. Theodosius marched west against the usurper Eugenius and his Frankish general Arbogast, who had raised the images of Jupiter and Hercules over their army. On the second day a fierce wind, the bora, blew dust into the faces of the Western army, and Theodosius won; it was the last stand of the old religion in arms.
    • The Empire Divided Between BrothersA.D. 395, Mediolanum, Italy. Theodosius died at Milan in January, the last emperor to rule both halves of the Roman world. The East passed to his elder son Arcadius at Constantinople, the West to the ten-year-old Honorius under the half-Vandal general Stilicho. The division, meant as a partnership, proved permanent.
    • Chandragupta II Conquers the Satrapsc. A.D. 400, Ujjain, Madhya Pradesh, India. Chandragupta II, called Vikramaditya, overthrew the last Saka satrap of Gujarat and Malwa, ending three centuries of Saka rule in the west and opening the Gupta realm to the ports of the Arabian Sea. He married his daughter Prabhavatigupta to the Vakataka king of the Deccan. His reign is remembered as the high noon of classical Indian civilization, the age of the poet Kalidasa.
    • The Yupa Stones of Kutaic. A.D. 400, Muara Kaman, Borneo. On the Mahakam River in eastern Borneo, King Mulavarman, grandson of Kundunga, raised stone sacrificial posts and inscribed them in Sanskrit to record his gift of twenty thousand cows to the Brahmans. These seven yupa are the oldest inscriptions in the Indonesian islands, and show Indian kingship, rites, and letters already rooted far from India by the end of the fourth century.
    • The Vandals Cross the RhineA.D. 406, Mogontiacum, Germania. On the last day of the year, by the traditional date, hosts of Vandals, Alans, and Suebi crossed the Rhine near Mogontiacum, overwhelmed the Franks who stood as Roman allies, and spread across Gaul. Within three years they had passed the Pyrenees into Spain; the Roman West never again held the Rhine.
    • Alaric Sacks RomeA.D. 410, Rome, Italy. After the execution of Stilicho left Italy without a defender, Alaric and the Visigoths besieged Rome three times, and on 24 August slaves opened the Salarian Gate. For three days the Goths plundered the city, though they spared the churches and those who fled to them. Rome had not fallen to a foreign enemy for eight hundred years, and the shock moved Augustine to write the City of God.
    • Augustine Writes the City of GodA.D. 413, Hippo Regius, Africa. Augustine, bishop of Hippo, once a teacher of rhetoric whose Confessions told of his conversion in a Milan garden, began his answer to the pagans who blamed the sack of Rome on the Christians. In twenty-two books he set the earthly city of self-love against the City of God; he died in 430 as the Vandals besieged Hippo.
    • The Stele of GwanggaetoA.D. 414, Ji'an, Jilin, China. King Jangsu raised a stone more than six metres tall at Gungnae to his father Gwanggaeto the Great, who had pushed Goguryeo's frontiers deep into Manchuria, humbled Baekje, and driven Wa raiders out of Silla. Its eighteen hundred characters, found again in the 1880s, are the oldest long history written in Korea, and scholars of three nations have quarrelled over their reading.
