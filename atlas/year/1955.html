    The chronicle of these years

    • The Death of StalinA.D. 1953, Kuntsevo, Moscow. Struck down by a stroke at his dacha at Kuntsevo, Stalin lay for hours before his frightened guards called doctors, and he died on 5 March 1953. Beria, Malenkov, and Khrushchev divided his power; Beria was arrested in June and shot. In East Berlin that June a workers' rising against raised work quotas was crushed by Soviet tanks.
    • The Fall of MossadeghA.D. 1953, Tehran, Iran. Mohammad Mossadegh, the elderly prime minister who nationalized the Anglo-Iranian Oil Company in 1951 to the acclaim of the Majlis and the crowds, defied the British embargo and the young Shah alike. In August 1953 a coup organized by the American and British intelligence services, with royalist officers and paid mobs, overthrew him. The Shah returned from Rome to rule with a firmer hand.
    • The Fall of Dien Bien PhuA.D. 1954, Dien Bien Phu, Vietnam. To draw the Viet Minh into a pitched battle, the French dropped paratroops into the valley of Dien Bien Phu in upper Tonkin. General Giap's men hauled artillery onto the surrounding hills by hand and opened their siege in March 1954; after fifty-six days the garrison of General de Castries was overrun on 7 May. At Geneva in July France gave up Indochina.
    • The All Saints' Day RisingA.D. 1954, Aurès Mountains, Algeria. On 1 November 1954 the National Liberation Front set off bombs and raids across Algeria, most of them in the Aurès mountains, beginning a war that would last nearly eight years. Algeria was French soil in law and home to a million European settlers; the war brought massacre and terror on both sides, torture by the army, and in 1958 the fall of the Fourth Republic.
    • The Bandung ConferenceA.D. 1955, Bandung, Java. In April 1955 Sukarno welcomed to Bandung the leaders of twenty-nine Asian and African nations, Nehru, Zhou Enlai, and Nasser among them, to declare the solidarity of the newly independent nations. Its ten principles of peaceful coexistence and its refusal to choose between the blocs of the Cold War foreshadowed the Non-Aligned Movement.
    • Khrushchev's Secret SpeechA.D. 1956, Great Kremlin Palace, Moscow. At a closed session on the last day of the Twentieth Party Congress, 25 February 1956, Nikita Khrushchev spoke for four hours on the crimes of Stalin: the cult of personality, the torture and murder of loyal communists, and the blunders of 1941. The text reached the West within weeks, shaking communist parties everywhere and emboldening Poland and Hungary.
    • The Hungarian RevolutionA.D. 1956, Parliament, Budapest. On 23 October 1956 Budapest students marching in sympathy with Poland pulled down the giant statue of Stalin, and the revolution spread across Hungary in days. Imre Nagy formed a government and on 1 November declared Hungary neutral and out of the Warsaw Pact. On 4 November Soviet tanks returned; some 2,500 Hungarians were killed, 200,000 fled west, and Nagy was hanged in 1958.
    • The Suez CrisisA.D. 1956, Port Said, Egypt. When America withdrew its offer to fund the Aswan High Dam, Nasser nationalized the Suez Canal Company in July 1956. By secret agreement Israel invaded Sinai on 29 October, and British and French paratroops landed at Port Said on 5 November. Under American and Soviet pressure they withdrew. Nasser, beaten in the field, emerged the hero of the Arab world, and British power in the region was broken.
