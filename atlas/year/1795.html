    The chronicle of these years

    • The First Fleet at Sydney CoveA.D. 1788, Sydney Cove, Australia. Eleven ships under Captain Arthur Phillip, carrying some fifteen hundred souls, more than half of them convicts, found Botany Bay unfit and moved to Port Jackson, where on 26 January 1788 the British flag was raised at Sydney Cove. The penal colony of New South Wales was the seed of Australia.
    • The Storming of the BastilleA.D. 1789, The Bastille, Paris. Three weeks after the Third Estate swore in a tennis court at Versailles to give France a constitution, with troops gathering around Paris and the popular minister Necker dismissed, crowds seeking powder stormed the Bastille, the royal fortress-prison of the city, on 14 July 1789. Its governor, de Launay, was murdered and his head paraded on a pike. Louis XVI yielded, and the French Revolution had begun.
    • The Cannonade of ValmyA.D. 1792, Valmy, Champagne. On 20 September 1792 the Prussian army, marching on Paris to rescue Louis XVI, came upon the French under Dumouriez and Kellermann at Valmy. The French line held under a long cannonade to cries of 'Long live the nation!', and the Duke of Brunswick withdrew. The next day the Convention abolished the monarchy. Goethe, present with the Prussians, wrote that a new epoch of world history began there.
    • Freetown FoundedA.D. 1792, Freetown, Sierra Leone. Some eleven hundred black Loyalists, who had won their freedom fighting for the Crown in the American war and been settled in Nova Scotia, sailed in fifteen ships under John Clarkson and founded Freetown on the Sierra Leone estuary. After Parliament abolished the slave trade in 1807, Freetown became a Crown colony and the base of the West Africa Squadron, where liberated Africans were landed by the thousand.
    • The Execution of Louis XVIA.D. 1793, Place de la Révolution, Paris. Tried by the National Convention and condemned by a narrow majority, Louis Capet, once Louis XVI, was guillotined in the Place de la Révolution on 21 January 1793. Britain, Holland, and Spain joined the war against France, the Vendée rose in March, and the Convention made terror the order of the day. Queen Marie Antoinette followed her husband to the scaffold in October.
    • The Fall of RobespierreA.D. 1794, Hôtel de Ville, Paris. In the summer of 1794, as the Terror reached its height with more than a thousand heads falling in Paris in seven weeks, the Convention turned on Maximilien Robespierre. On 9 Thermidor, 27 July, he was outlawed, his jaw shattered by a pistol shot at the Hôtel de Ville, and the next day he was guillotined with twenty-one of his followers. The Terror was over.
    • Agha Mohammad Khan Sacks TbilisiA.D. 1795, Krtsanisi, Georgia. Agha Mohammad Khan Qajar, a eunuch prince who had made himself master of Persia, demanded that King Heraclius II of Georgia renounce his Russian protection. When the aged king refused, the Persians routed his small army at Krtsanisi on 11 September 1795 and stormed Tbilisi. The city was burned and some fifteen thousand of its people carried off as slaves; Russia, which had promised aid, sent none in time.
    • Kamehameha at NuuanuA.D. 1795, Nuuanu Pali, Oahu. Kamehameha, chief of the island of Hawaii, armed with European muskets and cannon and aided by the seamen John Young and Isaac Davis, landed on Oahu and drove the army of Kalanikupule up the Nuuanu valley, where many were forced over the cliffs of the Pali. By 1810, when Kauai submitted, he ruled all the Hawaiian Islands as king.
