    The chronicle of these years

    • The Nuclear Tests of 1998A.D. 1998, Chagai, Balochistan, Pakistan. In May 1998 India exploded five nuclear devices beneath the desert at Pokhran, and two weeks later Pakistan answered with five of its own and then a sixth, beneath the Ras Koh hills of Chagai in Balochistan. Both nations declared themselves nuclear powers, and the old quarrel over Kashmir took on a new and terrible weight.
    • The Fall of SuhartoA.D. 1998, Jakarta, Indonesia. As prices soared, students occupied the parliament in Jakarta, and riots in May 1998 burned the Chinese quarters of the capital, killing more than a thousand. Abandoned by his ministers and the army, Suharto resigned on 21 May after thirty-two years in power. His successor B. J. Habibie freed the press, called elections, and allowed East Timor its vote.
    • NATO's War over KosovoA.D. 1999, Pristina, Kosovo. When Serbian forces under Milošević drove hundreds of thousands of Kosovo Albanians from their homes, NATO bombed Yugoslavia for seventy-eight days from 24 March 1999 until Belgrade withdrew its army in June and the province passed under United Nations rule. Milošević fell to a popular rising in October 2000 and died in 2006 while on trial at The Hague.
    • East Timor Votes for FreedomA.D. 1999, Dili, East Timor. On 30 August 1999, under the eyes of the United Nations, 78.5 percent of the East Timorese voted for independence. Militias armed by the Indonesian army then burned Dili and much of the country and drove a quarter of a million people into West Timor, until an Australian-led force landed on 20 September. East Timor became independent on 20 May 2002.
    • The Attacks of September 11A.D. 2001, New York, New York. On the morning of 11 September 2001, nineteen Islamist hijackers of al-Qaeda seized four American airliners, flew two into the towers of the World Trade Center, which collapsed, and one into the Pentagon; the fourth crashed in Pennsylvania as its passengers fought back. Nearly three thousand died. The United States invaded Afghanistan within a month and Iraq in 2003, beginning a twenty-year war on terror.
    • The Buddhas of Bamiyan DestroyedA.D. 2001, Bamiyan, Afghanistan. In March 2001 the Taliban, deaf to appeals from across the world and from Muslim scholars, declared the giant Buddhas of Bamiyan idols and destroyed them with artillery and explosives over several weeks. The empty niches, some fifty-five and thirty-eight metres high, still gape in the cliff where the statues had stood for fourteen centuries.
    • The Fall of the TalibanA.D. 2001, Kabul, Afghanistan. After the attacks of 11 September 2001, planned by al-Qaeda from its Afghan bases, the United States and Britain began bombing in October, and the Northern Alliance, whose leader Ahmad Shah Massoud had been murdered two days before the attacks, swept into Kabul on 13 November. In December the Bonn conference named Hamid Karzai to lead a new government.
    • The Euro in the PocketA.D. 2002, Frankfurt am Main, Germany. Created as the currency of eleven states on 1 January 1999 and managed by the European Central Bank at Frankfurt, the euro became money in the hand on 1 January 2002, when notes and coins replaced the mark, the franc, the lira, the guilder, the peseta, the drachma, and the other national currencies of twelve nations. The deutschmark, emblem of German recovery, passed away.
