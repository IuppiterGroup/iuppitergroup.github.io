    The chronicle of these years

    • The Constitution of the United StatesA.D. 1787, Philadelphia, Pennsylvania. Fifty-five delegates of twelve states met in the Pennsylvania State House through the summer of 1787, with George Washington presiding, and by the Great Compromise and long debate led by James Madison framed a federal government of three branches checking one another. Signed on 17 September and ratified the next year, it remains the oldest written national constitution in force.
    • The First Fleet at Sydney CoveA.D. 1788, Sydney Cove, Australia. Eleven ships under Captain Arthur Phillip, carrying some fifteen hundred souls, more than half of them convicts, found Botany Bay unfit and moved to Port Jackson, where on 26 January 1788 the British flag was raised at Sydney Cove. The penal colony of New South Wales was the seed of Australia; for the Eora and the peoples beyond, it was the beginning of the loss of their lands.
    • The Storming of the BastilleA.D. 1789, The Bastille, Paris. Three weeks after the Third Estate swore in a tennis court at Versailles to give France a constitution, with troops gathering around Paris and the popular minister Necker dismissed, crowds seeking powder stormed the Bastille, the royal fortress-prison of the city, on 14 July 1789. Its governor, de Launay, was murdered and his head paraded on a pike. Louis XVI yielded, and the French Revolution had begun.
    • The Declaration of the Rights of ManA.D. 1789, Versailles, France. In the night of 4 August 1789 the National Assembly abolished the feudal privileges of nobles, clergy, and provinces. On 26 August it adopted the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen: men are born and remain free and equal in rights, and sovereignty resides in the nation. In October a crowd of Parisian women marched to Versailles and brought the royal family back to Paris.
    • Quang Trung Routs the Qing at Dong DaA.D. 1789, Dong Da, Hanoi. When a Qing army under Sun Shiyi marched into Thang Long to restore the fugitive Le emperor, Nguyen Hue proclaimed himself Emperor Quang Trung and drove his army north by forced marches. On the fifth day of the lunar New Year of 1789 his troops, some behind shields of wet straw, stormed the Qing forts at Ngoc Hoi and Dong Da and sent the invaders flying back to China.
    • The Slaves of Saint-Domingue RiseA.D. 1791, Bois Caïman, Haiti. In the richest colony on earth, where half a million slaves produced much of Europe's sugar and coffee, the houngan Dutty Boukman gathered conspirators by night in the Bois Caïman in August 1791. Within days the northern plain was in flames, its plantations burned by the hundred and their masters slain; among the rebel leaders rose a freed coachman named Toussaint, later called Louverture.
    • The Cannonade of ValmyA.D. 1792, Valmy, Champagne. On 20 September 1792 the Prussian army, marching on Paris to rescue Louis XVI, came upon the French under Dumouriez and Kellermann at Valmy. The French line held under a long cannonade to cries of 'Long live the nation!', and the Duke of Brunswick withdrew. The next day the Convention abolished the monarchy. Goethe, present with the Prussians, wrote that a new epoch of world history began there.
    • Freetown FoundedA.D. 1792, Freetown, Sierra Leone. Some eleven hundred black Loyalists, who had won their freedom fighting for the Crown in the American war and been settled in Nova Scotia, sailed in fifteen ships under John Clarkson and founded Freetown on the Sierra Leone estuary. After Parliament abolished the slave trade in 1807, Freetown became a Crown colony and the base of the West Africa Squadron, where liberated Africans were landed by the thousand.
