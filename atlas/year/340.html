    The chronicle of these years

    • Jin Crosses the YangtzeA.D. 317, Jiankang, Jiangsu, China. After the capture of Emperor Min at Chang'an, Sima Rui, Prince of Langya, took the title King of Jin at Jiankang, and the next year the throne of the Eastern Jin. With the great families of the north fleeing south behind him, the Yangtze became the frontier of Chinese civilization, and the lands of the south were opened and peopled as never before.
    • Chandragupta I Founds the Gupta EraA.D. 320, Pataliputra, Bihar, India. Chandragupta, a chief of Magadha, married the Licchavi princess Kumaradevi, took the title of Great King of Kings, and began an era reckoned from A.D. 319 to 320. His gold coins show king and queen together, for the Licchavi alliance brought him power and prestige. From this beginning the Guptas built the empire of India's classical age.
    • The Council of NicaeaA.D. 325, Nicaea, Bithynia. Constantine summoned some three hundred bishops to Nicaea to settle the dispute raised by Arius, a priest of Alexandria who taught that the Son of God was a created being. The council declared the Son of one substance with the Father, fixed the common reckoning of Easter, and gave the Church its creed, recited ever since in the form enlarged at Constantinople in 381: the first council of the whole Church.
    • Constantinople DedicatedA.D. 330, Constantinople, Thrace. On 11 May Constantine dedicated New Rome on the site of Byzantium, a city of seven hills commanding the Bosporus and the roads between Europe and Asia, with a senate, a hippodrome, and a grain dole like the old capital. Constantinople was to be the capital of the Christian Roman Empire for eleven centuries and the bulwark of Christendom against the East.
    • Ezana of Axum Turns to Christc. A.D. 340, Axum, Ethiopia. Frumentius, a Syrian youth shipwrecked on the Red Sea coast and taken to the court of Axum, rose to be tutor of the young king Ezana, was consecrated bishop by Athanasius of Alexandria, and led him to the faith. Ezana's coins replaced the crescent and disc with the cross, among the first in the world to bear it.
    • The Allahabad Pillar of Samudraguptac. A.D. 350, Prayagraj, Uttar Pradesh, India. Samudragupta's court poet Harisena carved a eulogy of his master on an Ashokan pillar: he uprooted nine kings of the north, defeated twelve kings of the south from Kosala to Kanchi and then restored them, and took tribute from the frontier peoples. A player of the vina as well as a conqueror, Samudragupta performed the horse sacrifice and was hailed as without rival on earth.
    • Julian Dies in PersiaA.D. 363, Near Samarra, Mesopotamia. Julian, now emperor, who had renounced Christianity to restore the old gods, invaded Persia and reached Ctesiphon but could not take it. Retreating up the Tigris, he was struck by a spear in a skirmish and died that night. His successor Jovian bought the army's escape by ceding Nisibis and five provinces beyond the Tigris.
