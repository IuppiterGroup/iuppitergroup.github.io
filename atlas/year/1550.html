    The chronicle of these years

    • Copernicus Moves the EarthA.D. 1543, Frombork, Royal Prussia. Nicolaus Copernicus, canon of Frauenburg in Royal Prussia, had long worked out a system with the sun at rest near the center and the earth turning about it. His On the Revolutions of the Heavenly Spheres was printed at Nuremberg in 1543, and by tradition the first copy reached him on his deathbed. The same year Vesalius published his great anatomy of the human body.
    • The Silver Mountain of PotosíA.D. 1545, Potosí, Bolivia. An Indian herder named Diego Huallpa found silver on the Cerro Rico, the Rich Mountain, more than four thousand metres up in the Andes, and within a few decades Potosí was one of the largest cities in the world. Worked by Andean laborers drafted under the mita, its mines poured out silver that paid for Spain's wars, flowed through Manila to China, and bound the commerce of the world together.
    • The Council of Trent OpensA.D. 1545, Trent, Italy. On 13 December 1545 Pope Paul III's council opened at Trent, an imperial city on the road between Italy and Germany. In three periods of sitting over eighteen years it defined Catholic doctrine against the Protestants, upheld the seven sacraments and the Latin Vulgate, ordered seminaries for the training of priests, and set the Catholic Church upon the course it held for four centuries.
    • Francis Xavier in KagoshimaA.D. 1549, Kagoshima, Japan. The Jesuit Francis Xavier, guided by a Japanese convert named Anjirō, landed at Kagoshima and began to preach the Gospel in Japan, whose people he praised as the best yet discovered. In two years he founded the first Christian communities there, and within half a century Japan counted perhaps three hundred thousand Christians, among them great lords of Kyushu.
    • Salvador Founded in BrazilA.D. 1549, Salvador, Brazil. Tomé de Sousa, first governor-general of Brazil, arrived on the Bay of All Saints with a thousand soldiers, settlers, and convicts, and with the first Jesuits under Manuel da Nóbrega, and founded the city of Salvador as the capital of the colony. Ringed by sugar mills worked first by Indians and then by slaves from Africa, it remained the capital of Brazil until 1763.
    • Ivan the Terrible Takes KazanA.D. 1552, Kazan, Russia. Ivan IV, crowned in 1547 as the first Tsar of all Russia, laid siege to Kazan with a great army and sappers who mined its walls. On 2 October 1552 the mines blew breaches and the Russians stormed the city, slaughtering its defenders and freeing the Russian captives held there as slaves. Astrakhan fell four years later, the Volga became a Russian river, and St. Basil's rose on Red Square.
    • The Peace of AugsburgA.D. 1555, Augsburg, Germany. Exhausted by war, the princes of the Empire and King Ferdinand, acting for his brother Charles V, agreed at Augsburg on 25 September 1555 that each ruler should choose between the Catholic and the Lutheran faith for his own land, a rule later summed up as cuius regio, eius religio. Calvinists were left outside the peace, and Charles abdicated within the year.
    • The Second Battle of PanipatA.D. 1556, Panipat, Haryana, India. Hemu, a Hindu merchant who had risen to command the armies of the Sur Afghans, took Delhi and crowned himself Raja Vikramaditya. At Panipat he was winning against the Mughals of the fourteen-year-old Akbar when an arrow struck him in the eye. His leaderless army fled; Hemu was brought before Akbar and beheaded, and the Mughal Empire was restored for good.
