    The chronicle of these years

    • The Treaty of ZuhabA.D. 1639, Qasr-e Shirin, Persia. Murad IV, a sultan of iron severity, retook Baghdad from the Persians in December 1638 after a siege of forty days. The next May the two empires made peace at Zuhab near Qasr-e Shirin: Mesopotamia remained Ottoman, the Caucasus was divided, and the frontier drawn along the Zagros endures in substance as the border of Iran with Turkey and Iraq.
    • The Dutch Take MalaccaA.D. 1641, Malacca, Malaya. After a siege of five months, in which plague and hunger wasted the defenders, the Dutch East India Company, with the help of Johor, took Malacca from the Portuguese on 14 January 1641. The city that had been the emporium of the East sank into a quiet Dutch outpost, its trade drawn away to Batavia.
    • Tasman Sights New ZealandA.D. 1642, Golden Bay, New Zealand. Abel Tasman, sent from Batavia by Anthony van Diemen to find the Great South Land, sighted Tasmania in November 1642 and named it Van Diemen's Land. In December he raised the mountains of the South Island of New Zealand; at Golden Bay Maori canoes rammed his ship's boat and killed four seamen, and Tasman sailed away without landing.
    • The Fall of BeijingA.D. 1644, Peking, China. The rebel Li Zicheng broke into Beijing, and the Chongzhen Emperor, last of the Ming, hanged himself on Coal Hill behind the palace. The Ming general Wu Sangui, guarding the Shanhai Pass, opened the gate of the Great Wall to the Manchus, and together they routed Li Zicheng. The regent Dorgon entered Beijing and set the boy Shunzhi Emperor on the throne of China.
    • The Peace of WestphaliaA.D. 1648, Münster, Westphalia. After five years of negotiation at Münster and Osnabrück, the treaties signed in October 1648 ended the Thirty Years' War, which had cost Germany perhaps a fifth to a third of its people. Calvinists won recognition, and the princes of the Empire their sovereignty in all but name; Sweden and France took German lands, and the independence of the Dutch and the Swiss was confirmed. A new order of sovereign states began.
    • Khmelnytsky's RisingA.D. 1648, Korsun, Ukraine. Bohdan Khmelnytsky, a Cossack officer wronged by a Polish noble, allied with the Crimean Tatars and in May 1648 destroyed the Commonwealth's armies at Zhovti Vody and Korsun, taking both hetmans captive. Ukraine rose in a war of great savagery against Polish lords, Catholic priests, and Jews, many thousands of whom were slain. In 1654 at Pereyaslav the Cossacks swore allegiance to the tsar.
    • The Execution of Charles IA.D. 1649, Whitehall, London. Beaten at Naseby and handed over by the Scots, Charles I was tried by a High Court of Justice of Parliament's making and condemned as a tyrant, traitor, and murderer. On 30 January 1649 he stepped from a window of the Banqueting House onto a scaffold in Whitehall and died with dignity, and a groan went up from the crowd. England was declared a Commonwealth.
    • Van Riebeeck at the CapeA.D. 1652, Cape Town, South Africa. Jan van Riebeeck landed below Table Mountain with some ninety men of the Dutch East India Company to build a fort and garden to provision the fleets bound for the Indies. Free burghers soon took land along the Liesbeek River, slaves were brought from Angola, Madagascar, India, and the Indies, and the Khoikhoi, dispossessed of their grazing, were broken by war and by the smallpox of 1713.
