    The chronicle of these years

    • Luther's Ninety-Five ThesesA.D. 1517, Wittenberg, Saxony. On 31 October 1517 Martin Luther, Augustinian friar and professor of theology, sent his ninety-five theses against the sale of indulgences to the Archbishop of Mainz, and by tradition nailed them to the door of the castle church at Wittenberg. Printed and carried across Germany within weeks, they began the Reformation, which divided Western Christendom forever.
    • Charles I Licenses the African Slave TradeA.D. 1518, Santo Domingo, Hispaniola. With the Taíno of the Antilles dying of overwork and disease, Charles I licensed the shipment of four thousand African slaves to the Indies, the first great grant of what became the asiento; soon ships sailed direct from Guinea and São Tomé to the islands. In three and a half centuries some twelve and a half million Africans were carried in chains across the Atlantic, and nearly two million died on the way.
    • Tenochtitlan FallsA.D. 1521, Valley of Mexico. Hernán Cortés, with a few hundred Spaniards and tens of thousands of native allies, takes the Aztec capital of Tenochtitlan after a siege of nearly three months. The Aztec Empire falls, and on its ruins the Spaniards build Mexico City, capital of New Spain.
    • Magellan Falls at MactanA.D. 1521, Mactan, Philippines. Pressing the chiefs of the islands to submit to his ally Humabon, Ferdinand Magellan waded ashore at Mactan on 27 April 1521 with some fifty men against the warriors of Lapulapu, and was cut down in the shallows. Of his five ships only the Victoria, under Juan Sebastian Elcano, came home to Spain in 1522, completing the first voyage around the world.
    • The First Battle of PanipatA.D. 1526, Panipat, Haryana, India. Babur came down from Kabul with some twelve thousand men and met Ibrahim Lodi's army, perhaps eight times larger, at Panipat. Lashing carts together to shelter his matchlockmen and field guns, and sweeping round the flanks with mounted archers, he broke the Afghan host before midday. Ibrahim died on the field, and the Mughal Empire was born.
    • The Battle of MohácsA.D. 1526, Mohács, Hungary. On the marshy plain of Mohács on 29 August 1526 the Hungarian army of King Louis II, some twenty-five thousand men, charged Suleiman's far greater host and was annihilated in two hours. The king drowned in a stream as he fled, and seven bishops lay among the dead. Suleiman entered Buda, and the kingdom of Hungary was broken into Ottoman, Habsburg, and Transylvanian parts.
    • The Sack of RomeA.D. 1527, Rome, Italy. On 6 May 1527 the unpaid army of Charles V, Spanish soldiers and Lutheran landsknechts, stormed Rome after its general, the Constable of Bourbon, was killed on the walls. For weeks they murdered, tortured, and plundered, while Pope Clement VII watched from the Castel Sant'Angelo. The sack ended the High Renaissance in Rome and bound the papacy to the emperor.
    • Pizarro Seizes Atahualpa at CajamarcaA.D. 1532, Cajamarca, Peru. Fresh from his victory over his brother Huascar in a civil war that had torn the Inca empire, Atahualpa met Francisco Pizarro's 168 Spaniards in the square of Cajamarca on 16 November 1532. At a signal the Spaniards fell upon his unarmed retinue with guns, horses, and steel, killing thousands, and took him captive; he filled a room with gold for his ransom and was garroted the following July.
