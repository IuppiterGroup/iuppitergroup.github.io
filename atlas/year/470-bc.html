    The chronicle of these years

    • The Athenians at Marathon490 B.C., Marathon, Greece. Darius sent Datis and Artaphernes across the Aegean to punish Athens. On the plain of Marathon some 10,000 Athenians and 1,000 Plataeans under Miltiades charged at a run, broke the Persian wings, and drove the invaders to their ships, losing 192 men against 6,400 Persians. The runner Pheidippides had gone to Sparta for help, which came too late.
    • Thermopylae and Salamis480 B.C., Thermopylae, Greece. Leonidas and three hundred Spartans, with their Greek allies, hold the pass at Thermopylae against the host of Xerxes for three days. Betrayed by a mountain path, the Spartans fight to the death. Weeks later the Athenian fleet destroys the Persian navy in the strait of Salamis, and Greece is saved.
    • Gelon Defeats Carthage at Himera480 B.C., Himera, Sicily. On the same day as Salamis, it was said, Gelon of Syracuse and Theron of Akragas destroyed a huge Carthaginian army under Hamilcar at Himera. Hamilcar was killed or cast himself into the sacrificial fire, and the captives were set to build the temples of Akragas. The victory kept Sicily Greek for generations.
    • Ajatashatru Crushes the Vajjisc. 480 B.C., Vaishali, Bihar, India. Ajatashatru, who had seized the throne of Magadha and starved his father Bimbisara in prison, waged a long war on the Vajji league of republics north of the Ganges. Jain texts tell of a great stone-hurling engine and a chariot fitted with whirling maces. To guard the river crossing he built a fort at the village of Pataligrama, seed of Pataliputra.
    • The Death of Confucius479 B.C., Qufu, Shandong, China. Kong Qiu, Master Kong, died in Lu at seventy-three, never having found a prince who would put his teaching into practice. He taught that order rested on ritual, filial duty, and the cultivation of humane virtue in rulers and ministers alike. His disciples set down his sayings in the Analects, and his teaching became the moral foundation of China, Korea, Vietnam, and Japan.
    • The Battle of Plataea479 B.C., Plataea, Greece. The Spartan regent Pausanias led the largest Greek army ever assembled, nearly 40,000 hoplites, against the Persian general Mardonius below Mount Cithaeron. The Spartans and Tegeans broke the Persian charge, Mardonius fell, and the camp was taken. On the same day, tradition says, the Greeks burned the Persian fleet at Mycale, and the invasions of Greece were over.
    • The Three Families Destroy Zhi453 B.C., Jinyang, Shanxi, China. Zhi Bo, strongest of the ministers who ruled Jin, besieged Zhao Xiangzi in Jinyang and flooded the city until frogs bred in the cooking pots. But the Han and Wei clans secretly turned against him, broke the dikes onto his own camp, and with Zhao destroyed him. The three families divided Jin among themselves, and the age of the Warring States began.
    • The Twelve Tables451 B.C., Forum, Rome. To end the patricians' hold on unwritten law, the Romans suspended the consuls and chose ten men, the decemvirs, to write the law down. Their Twelve Tables, set up in bronze in the Forum, governed debt, family, property, and crime, and Roman boys learned them by heart for centuries: the root of the civil law of Europe.
