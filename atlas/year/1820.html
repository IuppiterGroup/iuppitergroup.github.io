    The chronicle of these years

    • San Martín Crosses the AndesA.D. 1817, Chacabuco, Chile. José de San Martín led the Army of the Andes, some five thousand men with mules and cannon, over passes higher than twelve thousand feet from Mendoza into Chile in January 1817, and with Bernardo O'Higgins routed the royalists at Chacabuco on 12 February. A year later the victory of Maipú made Chile free, and San Martín turned north by sea against the Spanish citadel of Peru.
    • Ibrahim Pasha Razes DiriyahA.D. 1818, Diriyah, Najd. At the sultan's command Muhammad Ali sent his son Ibrahim Pasha into Arabia to destroy the Wahhabi state. After a march across the peninsula Ibrahim besieged Diriyah for six months and took it in September 1818. The town was razed, and the emir Abdullah ibn Saud was sent to Constantinople, paraded through the streets, and beheaded. Within six years the Saudis had raised a new capital at Riyadh.
    • Raffles Founds SingaporeA.D. 1819, Singapore. Seeking a British port to break the Dutch hold on the Straits, Raffles landed on the island of Singapore on 29 January 1819, then home to perhaps a thousand Malays, Orang Laut, and Chinese under the Temenggong. Playing on a disputed succession, he recognized Hussein as Sultan of Johor and won the right to a trading post. Free of duties, Singapore grew within years into the emporium of the East.
    • The Rise of ShakaA.D. 1819, kwaBulawayo, Zululand. Shaka, chief of the small Zulu clan since 1816, drilled his regiments to close with the short stabbing spear behind the great cowhide shield, in the horns-of-the-buffalo formation. After he defeated Zwide of the Ndwandwe on the Mhlatuze River in 1819, he ruled the land between the Phongolo and the Thukela, and the wars of the age, the Mfecane, set peoples in flight across half of southern Africa.
    • Bolívar at BoyacáA.D. 1819, Boyacá Bridge, Colombia. Simón Bolívar, the Caracas aristocrat who had sworn on Rome's Monte Sacro to free his country, marched his army across the flooded llanos and over the frozen páramo of Pisba in the rainy season to fall upon New Granada. At the bridge of Boyacá on 7 August 1819 he destroyed the royalist army; three days later he entered Bogotá, and in December the Republic of Colombia was proclaimed.
    • The Army of the Three GuaranteesA.D. 1821, Mexico City, Mexico. Agustín de Iturbide, a royalist colonel who had spent years hunting insurgents, changed sides, joined the rebel Vicente Guerrero under the Plan of Iguala, which promised independence, the Catholic faith, and the union of Spaniards and Mexicans, and entered Mexico City in triumph on 27 September 1821. The next year he had himself crowned Emperor Agustín I; he was overthrown in 1823 and shot in 1824.
    • The Massacre of ChiosA.D. 1822, Chios, Aegean. When the Greeks of the Morea rose in 1821 and slaughtered the Muslims and Jews of Tripolitsa, the sultan's vengeance fell elsewhere. In April 1822, after a band of Samians raised revolt on the rich island of Chios, an Ottoman fleet landed troops who killed perhaps twenty thousand Christians and sold some forty-five thousand into slavery. Delacroix's painting of the massacre roused all Europe for the Greek cause.
    • Independence or DeathA.D. 1822, Ipiranga, São Paulo. Ordered by the Cortes at Lisbon to return to Portugal while Brazil was reduced again to a colony, the Prince Regent Pedro, left behind by his father John VI, refused. On 7 September 1822, on the banks of the Ipiranga stream near São Paulo, he tore the Portuguese colors from his uniform and cried 'Independence or death!'; in December he was crowned Emperor Pedro I of Brazil.
