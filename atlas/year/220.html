    The chronicle of these years

    • Nagarjuna and the Middle Wayc. A.D. 200, Amaravati, Andhra Pradesh, India. Nagarjuna, a monk of the Andhra country in the days of the later Satavahanas, taught that all things are empty of fixed being, arising only in dependence upon one another. His Madhyamaka, the philosophy of the Middle Way, became a foundation of Mahayana thought in Tibet, China, and Japan. In the same land the great stupa of Amaravati was sheathed in carved limestone slabs.
    • The Pyramid of the Sunc. A.D. 200, Teotihuacan, Mexico. In the northeast of the Valley of Mexico, the builders of Teotihuacan completed the Pyramid of the Sun beside their Avenue of the Dead, in a city laid out on a grid that would house perhaps a hundred thousand people, the greatest of the Americas. Its merchants and soldiers carried the cult of the Feathered Serpent and the storm god as far as the Maya lowlands; its true name and tongue are lost.
    • The Battle of Red CliffsA.D. 208, Chibi, Hubei, China. Cao Cao, master of the north, came down the Yangtze with a great army to finish Sun Quan and Liu Bei. At the Red Cliffs, Huang Gai sent fire ships loaded with oil and dry reeds into the crowded enemy fleet, and as a southeast wind spread the blaze Cao Cao's host fled north through the marshes, wasted by fever. China would stay divided for seventy years.
    • Citizenship for All Free MenA.D. 212, Rome, Italy. By the Constitutio Antoniniana the emperor Caracalla granted Roman citizenship to nearly all free inhabitants of the empire. Dio says he did it to make more men liable to the inheritance tax; but henceforth the peoples of the Mediterranean, from Britain to Syria, called themselves Romans, and the name endured in the East for a thousand years.
    • The End of the HanA.D. 220, Xuchang, Henan, China. Months after the death of Cao Cao, his son Cao Pi had the puppet Emperor Xian yield the throne to him on a platform near Xu, and proclaimed the Wei dynasty. Liu Bei answered by declaring himself emperor of Han at Chengdu, and Sun Quan later took the imperial title in the south. The four centuries of Han were over, and the Three Kingdoms began.
    • Ardashir Crowned at CtesiphonA.D. 226, Ctesiphon, Mesopotamia. Ardashir, a prince of Persis, overthrew and killed the last Parthian king, Artabanus IV, on the plain of Hormozgan and was crowned King of Kings of Iran at Ctesiphon. His Sasanian dynasty made Zoroastrianism the faith of the realm, laid claim to the old Achaemenid lands, and fought Rome for four centuries.
    • The Queen of Wa Sends EnvoysA.D. 239, Luoyang, Henan, China. Himiko, a priestess queen who ruled the Wa from her palace at Yamatai, unseen by her people and served by a thousand women, sent envoys by way of Daifang to the Wei court. Wei named her Queen of Wa, Friend of Wei, and sent her a gold seal, a hundred bronze mirrors, and silks. Whether Yamatai lay in Kyushu or in the Yamato plain is still disputed.
    • Persia Takes the Kushan Westc. A.D. 240, Bactra (Balkh), Afghanistan. The Sasanian kings Ardashir I and Shapur I carried Persian arms over the Kushan lands of Bactria and the Kabul valley; Shapur's inscription at Naqsh-e Rustam names Kushanshahr as far as Peshawar among his provinces. Sasanian princes ruled there as Kushanshahs, striking coins in the Kushan manner, while the old Kushan line lingered in the Punjab and at Mathura.
