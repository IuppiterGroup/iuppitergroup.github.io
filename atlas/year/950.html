    The chronicle of these years

    • Athelstan at BrunanburhA.D. 937, Brunanburh, England. Athelstan of Wessex, first king to rule all the English, met a grand alliance of Olaf Guthfrithson of Dublin, Constantine II of the Scots, and Owain of Strathclyde. In the greatest slaughter yet seen on the island, the Chronicle sang, the invaders were broken. The field is still disputed, perhaps on the Wirral, but the victory confirmed the making of England.
    • Ngo Quyen at the Bach Dang RiverA.D. 938, Bach Dang River, Vietnam. Ngo Quyen planted iron-tipped stakes in the bed of the Bach Dang and, when the tide ran in, lured the war junks of the Southern Han upstream. As the water fell the fleet was impaled and broken, and the Han prince Liu Hongcao was slain. The victory ended a thousand years of Chinese rule, and Ngo Quyen made himself king at Co Loa.
    • The Buyids Enter BaghdadA.D. 945, Baghdad, Iraq. Ahmad ibn Buya, one of three Daylamite brothers from the mountains south of the Caspian, entered Baghdad in December 945. The caliph al-Mustakfi gave him the title Mu'izz al-Dawla and was blinded and deposed within weeks. For a hundred and ten years the Shi'ite Buyid amirs ruled Iraq and western Persia, keeping the Sunni caliphs as revered prisoners in their own palace.
    • The Battle of TakkolamA.D. 949, Takkolam, Tamil Nadu, India. The Rashtrakuta emperor Krishna III, with his Western Ganga ally Butuga II, crushed the Chola army at Takkolam. The Chola crown prince Rajaditya was slain on his elephant by an arrow from Butuga. Krishna III occupied the country about Kanchi, styling himself conqueror of Kanchi and Tanjore, and the Cholas did not recover their strength for a generation.
    • The Toltecs at Tulac. A.D. 950, Tula, Mexico. On a ridge above the Tula River, the Toltecs built Tollan, a city of perhaps sixty thousand, whose temple roof was borne by colossal stone warriors with butterfly breastplates and spear-throwers. Later peoples remembered the Toltecs as masters of all the arts and their priest-king Topiltzin Quetzalcoatl as a god; the Aztec kings claimed descent from them.
    • Otto the Great at the LechfeldA.D. 955, Lechfeld, Bavaria. On 10 August 955 a Magyar host besieging Augsburg met Otto I and the levies of every German duchy, marching beneath the Holy Lance. The Magyars were routed and hunted down at the river crossings, and their leaders were hanged at Regensburg. Their raids on the West ended forever, and Otto's victory won him the renown that carried him to Rome.
    • The Mutiny at ChenqiaoA.D. 960, Chenqiao, Henan, China. Marching north against the Khitan, the officers of the Later Zhou palace army halted at Chenqiao, wrapped their commander Zhao Kuangyin in a yellow robe, and hailed him emperor. He returned to Kaifeng, received the throne from the boy emperor, and founded the Song. Later, over wine, he persuaded his generals to retire to rich estates, and the soldier was made servant to the scholar for a thousand years.
    • Otto I Crowned EmperorA.D. 962, St. Peter's, Rome. On 2 February 962 Pope John XII crowned Otto, King of the Germans and of Italy, as emperor in St. Peter's, and the imperial dignity, vacant in the West for nearly forty years, passed to the German kings. The empire later called Holy and Roman would endure until 1806.
