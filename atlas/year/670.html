    The chronicle of these years

    • The Fall of BaekjeA.D. 660, Sabi, Korea. A Tang fleet under Su Dingfang landed at the mouth of the Geum River while the Silla general Kim Yu-sin marched from the east. At Hwangsanbeol the Baekje general Gyebaek, who had killed his own family rather than see them enslaved, fell with his five thousand. Sabi was taken, King Uija was led captive to Chang'an, and Baekje was no more.
    • Ali Struck Down at KufaA.D. 661, Kufa, Iraq. As he entered the mosque of Kufa for the dawn prayer in January 661, Ali was struck with a poisoned sword by the Kharijite Ibn Muljam, and he died two days later. His son Hasan soon gave up the caliphate to Mu'awiya, who ruled from Damascus as the first caliph of the house of Umayya. The Shi'a, the party of Ali, venerate his tomb at Najaf.
    • The Battle of BaekgangA.D. 663, Gunsan, Korea. Yamato sent fleets and tens of thousands of men to restore Baekje. At the mouth of the Baekgang the Tang navy, though fewer, caught the Japanese ships in the narrows and burned four hundred of them. Japan withdrew from the peninsula and began to build fortresses at home against a Tang invasion that never came.
    • The Synod of WhitbyA.D. 664, Whitby, England. At the abbey of the abbess Hilda, King Oswiu of Northumbria heard the Irish party of Bishop Colman and the Roman party of Wilfrid dispute the reckoning of Easter and the form of the tonsure. When Wilfrid declared that Peter held the keys of heaven, the king chose Rome, and the English Church was bound thenceforth to the Roman order rather than to the customs of Iona.
    • The Fall of GoguryeoA.D. 668, Pyongyang, Korea. After the death of the dictator Yeon Gaesomun his sons fell to quarrelling, and the eldest went over to Tang. The armies of Li Ji and of Silla closed on Pyongyang, which fell after a month's siege, and King Bojang was carried to Chang'an. Goguryeo had lasted seven centuries; within eight years Silla had driven the Tang from the peninsula south of the Taedong.
    • Uqba Founds KairouanA.D. 670, Kairouan, Ifriqiya. Uqba ibn Nafi planted a fortified camp in the Tunisian plain, out of reach of the Roman fleet, as a base for the conquest of the Maghreb. He called it al-Qayrawan, the camp, and laid out its Great Mosque. Kairouan became the capital of Ifriqiya, a seat of learning, and in the eyes of the Maghreb the holiest city after Mecca, Medina, and Jerusalem.
    • Greek Fire Saves Constantinoplec. A.D. 678, Constantinople, Thrace. For several summers the fleets of the Caliph Mu'awiya pressed upon Constantinople from a base at Cyzicus. Against them Constantine IV turned a new weapon, the liquid fire of Kallinikos, an architect who had fled from Syria, pumped through bronze siphons onto the enemy ships. The Arabs withdrew with heavy loss, and the caliph bought a thirty-year peace with tribute.
    • The Martyrdom at KarbalaA.D. 680, Karbala, Iraq. Husayn, grandson of the Prophet, set out from Mecca to lead the Kufans against the new caliph Yazid. On the plain of Karbala, cut off from the Euphrates, his band of some seventy men was surrounded by the governor's troops and slain on the tenth of Muharram, October 680. His head was carried to Damascus. The Shi'a have kept that day of Ashura in mourning ever since.
