    The chronicle of these years

    • The Maccabees Rise167 B.C., Modein, Judea. When Antiochus IV forbade the Jewish law and set up an altar to Olympian Zeus in the Temple, the priest Mattathias killed a royal officer at Modein and fled to the hills with his five sons. His son Judas Maccabeus beat the Seleucid generals and in 164 cleansed and rededicated the Temple, a deliverance still kept at Hanukkah.
    • Dutugemunu Defeats Elarac. 161 B.C., Anuradhapura, Sri Lanka. Dutugemunu, prince of Ruhuna in the south of Lanka, marched north against Elara, a Chola from the mainland who had ruled at Anuradhapura for forty-four years. The chronicle tells that the two kings met in single combat on their elephants beneath the city's walls and Elara fell. Dutugemunu honoured his foe with a monument and raised the great Ruwanwelisaya stupa.
    • King Milinda Questions Nagasenac. 150 B.C., Sagala (Sialkot), Pakistan. Menander, greatest of the Indo-Greek kings, ruled from Sagala in the Punjab and pressed his arms toward the Ganges. The Pali book Milindapanha preserves his dialogues with the monk Nagasena on the self, rebirth, and the chariot that is nothing but its parts. Plutarch says that when he died the cities quarrelled over his ashes and shared them among themselves.
    • Carthage Destroyed146 B.C., Carthage, Africa. After a siege of three years, Scipio Aemilianus storms Carthage, burns it street by street, and sells its survivors into slavery. For years Cato had closed his speeches in the Senate with one demand: Carthage must be destroyed. Corinth falls the same year, and Rome stands without a rival in the Mediterranean.
    • Mummius Destroys Corinth146 B.C., Corinth, Greece. When the Achaean League defied Rome, the consul Lucius Mummius defeated its army at the Isthmus and destroyed Corinth, killing its men, selling its women and children, and shipping its statues and paintings to Rome, warning the shippers, it was said, that any masterpiece they lost they must replace with a new one. In the same year Carthage fell, and Greece passed under Roman rule.
    • Mithridates I Takes Babylonia141 B.C., Seleucia on the Tigris, Mesopotamia. Mithridates I of Parthia, having taken Media from the Seleucids, entered Seleucia on the Tigris, and the scribes of Babylon began to date their tablets by his reign. Three years later his generals captured the Seleucid king Demetrius II, and Parthia was master of the Iranian plateau and Mesopotamia; its kings made Ctesiphon, across the river, their winter capital.
    • Zhang Qian Goes West138 B.C., Chang'an, Shaanxi, China. Emperor Wu sent the courtier Zhang Qian to find the Yuezhi and win them to an alliance against the Xiongnu. Captured on the way, he lived ten years among the Xiongnu and took a wife, then escaped and reached Ferghana and Bactria. He came home in 126 B.C. with news of blood-sweating horses, vines, and unknown kingdoms, and opened the road later called the Silk Road.
    • The Murder of Tiberius Gracchus133 B.C., Capitol, Rome. The tribune Tiberius Sempronius Gracchus, grandson of Scipio Africanus, passed a law to share out the public land among the poor and, when blocked, had a fellow tribune deposed. Seeking a second term, he was clubbed to death on the Capitol with some three hundred of his followers by senators led by the chief pontiff Scipio Nasica: the first blood of the strife that ended the Republic.
