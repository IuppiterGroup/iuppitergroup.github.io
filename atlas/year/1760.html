    The chronicle of these years

    • The Treaty of GiyantiA.D. 1755, Giyanti, Java. After years of war over the succession of Mataram, the Dutch Company divided the realm of Sultan Agung by the Treaty of Giyanti. Pakubuwono III kept Surakarta, while his uncle and rival Mangkubumi became Sultan Hamengkubuwono I of Yogyakarta. The two courts, rivals in ceremony and dance, remained the guardians of Javanese high culture.
    • The Battle of PlasseyA.D. 1757, Palashi, West Bengal, India. Robert Clive, with some three thousand men, a third of them European, met the army of Siraj ud-Daulah, nawab of Bengal, fifty thousand strong, in a mango grove at Plassey. Rain soaked the nawab's powder while the British kept theirs dry, and Mir Jafar, the nawab's commander bought by the Company, held his troops aloof. Siraj fled and was murdered; the East India Company became master of Bengal.
    • The Destruction of the DzungarsA.D. 1757, Ili, Xinjiang, China. The Qianlong Emperor, called in by the Dzungar prince Amursana, conquered the Dzungar Khanate in 1755, and when Amursana rose against him he ordered the nation destroyed. Qing soldiers, smallpox, and famine killed the greater part of the Dzungar people, and by 1759 the Qing held the oases of the Tarim as well. The new conquests came to be called Xinjiang, the New Frontier.
    • Wolfe and Montcalm at QuebecA.D. 1759, Quebec, Canada. In the night of 12 to 13 September 1759, General James Wolfe's army climbed a goat path up the cliffs above the St. Lawrence and formed on the Plains of Abraham outside Quebec. The Marquis de Montcalm attacked, and in a quarter of an hour the British volleys broke the French line; both generals were mortally wounded, Quebec surrendered, and the Treaty of Paris of 1763 gave Canada to Britain.
    • The Third Battle of PanipatA.D. 1761, Panipat, Haryana, India. The Maratha army under Sadashivrao Bhau, marching north to defend Delhi, was penned at Panipat by Ahmad Shah Durrani and starved for weeks. On 14 January 1761 it attacked and was destroyed; the Peshwa's son Vishwasrao and the Bhau fell, and tens of thousands of soldiers and camp followers were slaughtered or carried into slavery in the pursuit. The Maratha drive to rule all India was broken.
    • The British Take ManilaA.D. 1762, Manila, Philippines. In the Seven Years' War a British fleet from Madras under Admiral Samuel Cornish and General William Draper stormed Manila in October 1762. Archbishop Manuel Rojo, the acting governor, surrendered the city, but Simon de Anda withdrew to Bacolor in Pampanga and kept up the war in the provinces. Confined to Manila, the British handed it back in 1764.
    • The Battle of BuxarA.D. 1764, Buxar, Bihar, India. Mir Qasim, the deposed nawab of Bengal, joined Shuja ud-Daula of Awadh and the Mughal emperor Shah Alam II against the Company. At Buxar on the Ganges Major Hector Munro, with about seven thousand men, routed their forty thousand. The emperor came over to the British, and the next year at Allahabad granted the Company the diwani, the right to collect the revenue of Bengal, Bihar, and Orissa.
    • The Burmese Destroy AyutthayaA.D. 1767, Ayutthaya, Thailand. After a siege of fourteen months, the armies of Hsinbyushin of Burma stormed Ayutthaya on 7 April 1767. The city of four centuries was burned, its temples stripped of their gold, its king Ekkathat dead, and tens of thousands carried off to Burma. Yet within months a general of Chinese descent, Taksin, had gathered an army in the southeast to win Siam back.
