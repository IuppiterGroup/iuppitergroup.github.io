    The chronicle of these years

    • The First Emperor221 B.C., Xianyang, China. King Zheng of Qin swallows the last of the Warring States and takes a new title, Qin Shi Huang, the First Emperor. He standardizes script, coinage, weights, and axle widths, joins the northern walls, and goes to his tomb guarded by an army of terracotta soldiers.
    • Hannibal Crosses the Alps218 B.C., Western Alps. Hannibal led an army of Spaniards and Africans, with thirty-seven elephants, over the Pyrenees and the Rhone and in late autumn across the Alps, fighting mountain tribes and snow. He came down into the plain of the Po with some 26,000 men, beat the Romans at the Trebia in December, and won the Gauls of the north to his side.
    • Cannae216 B.C., Apulia, Italy. Hannibal draws a Roman army of some eighty thousand into his center at Cannae, closes the wings, and annihilates it in a single day, one of the bloodiest in the history of war. Rome refuses to ask for terms. It raises new legions and fights on.
    • Meng Tian and the Long Wall214 B.C., Ordos, Inner Mongolia, China. The general Meng Tian, with three hundred thousand men, drove the Xiongnu from the Ordos loop of the Yellow River and joined the walls of Qin, Zhao, and Yan into a single rampart running ten thousand li from Lintao to Liaodong. A straight road was cut from the capital to the frontier, and the toil of the conscripts on the wall became a byword for suffering.
    • The Burning of the Books213 B.C., Xianyang, Shaanxi, China. On the advice of the chancellor Li Si, the First Emperor ordered the burning of the histories of the conquered states and of private copies of the Odes and the Documents, sparing only works on medicine, divination, and farming. The next year, tradition says, some 460 scholars were buried alive. Later Confucians remembered Qin chiefly for this assault on learning.
    • The First Emperor Dies at Shaqiu210 B.C., Shaqiu, Hebei, China. Seeking the elixir of immortality on a tour of the east, the First Emperor died at Shaqiu. The eunuch Zhao Gao and the chancellor Li Si hid his death, carting salted fish beside his litter to cover the stench, and forged an edict ordering the heir Fusu to kill himself. The younger son Huhai took the throne as Second Emperor, and the dynasty began its swift collapse.
    • The Terracotta Armyc. 210 B.C., Mount Li, Shaanxi, China. Beside the First Emperor's mound at Mount Li were buried some eight thousand soldiers of fired clay, each with his own face, drawn up in battle order with real bronze weapons, chariots, and horses. Sima Qian wrote that 700,000 convicts built the tomb, with rivers of mercury and crossbows set to shoot robbers. Peasants digging a well found the army in 1974.
    • Chen Sheng Rises at Dazexiang209 B.C., Dazexiang, Anhui, China. Two conscript leaders, Chen Sheng and Wu Guang, held up by rain on the road to the frontier, knew that lateness meant death under the laws of Qin. They killed their officers and raised the cry: are kings and nobles a breed apart? Their rising lasted barely six months, but it set the whole empire ablaze.
