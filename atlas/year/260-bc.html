    The chronicle of these years

    • Rome Crosses to Messana264 B.C., Messana, Sicily. The Mamertines, Campanian mercenaries who had seized Messana, appealed to Rome against Hiero of Syracuse and Carthage. The consul Appius Claudius Caudex crossed the strait, and Rome for the first time sent an army out of Italy. The First Punic War had begun, a struggle of twenty-three years for Sicily and the sea.
    • Ashoka Conquers Kalingac. 261 B.C., Dhauli, Odisha, India. Eight years after his consecration, the Mauryan emperor Ashoka conquered Kalinga on the eastern coast. His own edict counts 150,000 carried away, 100,000 slain, and many times that number dead. Struck by remorse, he turned to the Buddha's teaching and proclaimed a rule of dhamma, carving his edicts on rocks and pillars across the empire and sending envoys to the Greek kings.
    • The Slaughter at Changping260 B.C., Gaoping, Shanxi, China. After long deadlock Zhao replaced its cautious general Lian Po with young Zhao Kuo, who knew the books of war by heart. Bai Qi of Qin feigned retreat, cut off his army, and starved it for forty-six days. When Zhao Kuo fell, the Qin buried the surrendered men alive, by the old count four hundred thousand, and Zhao never recovered.
    • An Duong Vuong Builds Co Loac. 257 B.C., Co Loa, Vietnam. Thuc Phan, a prince from the hill country to the north, overthrew the last Hung king, took the name An Duong Vuong, and founded the kingdom of Au Lac. At Co Loa he raised a citadel of three spiraling earthen ramparts, the largest fortress of ancient Southeast Asia; a hoard of ten thousand bronze arrowheads was dug from its ground in 1959.
    • Li Bing Tames the Min Riverc. 256 B.C., Dujiangyan, Sichuan, China. Li Bing, the Qin governor of Shu, split the Min River with an artificial island shaped like a fish's mouth and cut a channel through the mountain to carry its waters onto the Chengdu plain. Without a single dam, the work ended the floods and watered a plain that became the Land of Abundance, and it waters that plain to this day.
    • Mahinda Brings the Dhamma to Lankac. 250 B.C., Mihintale, Sri Lanka. Ashoka's son Mahinda, a monk, met King Devanampiya Tissa on the hill of Mihintale while the king was hunting deer, and converted him to the Buddha's teaching. Mahinda's sister Sanghamitta followed with a cutting of the Bodhi tree, planted at Anuradhapura, where its descendant grows still. Theravada Buddhism has been the faith of the Sinhalese ever since.
    • Diodotus Breaks with the Seleucidsc. 250 B.C., Bactra (Balkh), Afghanistan. Diodotus, satrap of Bactria, threw off allegiance to the Seleucid king and founded a Greek kingdom between the Hindu Kush and the Oxus, rich in irrigated grain and the caravan trade. Justin calls it the land of a thousand cities. Its kings struck some of the finest coins of the Hellenistic world, portraits of startling realism above Zeus hurling the thunderbolt.
    • Jenne-jeno on the Nigerc. 250 B.C., Jenne-jeno, Mali. On a mound in the floodplain of the Bani, a tributary of the Niger, settlers founded the town that grew into Jenne-jeno, ancient Djenné. By about A.D. 800 it was a walled town of many thousands, trading iron, copper, grain, and fish along the river, and rich long before the first Arab caravan crossed the desert.
