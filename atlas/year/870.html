    The chronicle of these years

    • Rurik Called to NovgorodA.D. 862, Novgorod, Rus'. The Primary Chronicle tells how the Slav and Finnic tribes of the north, weary of strife among themselves, sent across the sea to the Varangian Rus': 'Our land is great and rich, but there is no order in it. Come and rule over us.' Rurik and his brothers came, and Rurik's house would rule in Rus' and Muscovy until 1598.
    • Cyril and Methodius in MoraviaA.D. 863, Velehrad, Moravia. At the request of Prince Rastislav, who wished for teachers in his people's own tongue, Michael III sent two brothers from Thessalonica, Constantine, called Cyril, and Methodius. For the Slavs they devised the Glagolitic alphabet and rendered the Gospels and the liturgy into Slavonic, laying the foundation of the written culture of the Orthodox Slavs.
    • The Great Heathen ArmyA.D. 865, East Anglia, England. A host of Danes, which the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle called the great army, landed in East Anglia in 865, led, tradition says, by the sons of Ragnar Lodbrok, Ivar the Boneless and Halfdan. Within five years they had taken York, slain the kings of Northumbria and King Edmund of East Anglia, and broken both kingdoms; of the English realms only Wessex would stand.
    • The Diamond Sutra PrintedA.D. 868, Dunhuang, Gansu, China. On a scroll five metres long, a man named Wang Jie had the Diamond Sutra printed from carved wooden blocks for free distribution, on behalf of his parents. Found in the sealed library cave at Dunhuang, it is the oldest printed book that bears a date. Under the Song, printing spread to the classics, the histories, and paper money, and transformed learning in East Asia.
    • The Zanj Sack BasraA.D. 871, Basra, Iraq. On the salt flats below Basra, thousands of East African slaves, the Zanj, shipped up the Gulf by Arab and Persian traders, toiled in gangs to clear the soil for the planters. In 869 a pretender named Ali ibn Muhammad roused them to revolt, and in September 871 the Zanj stormed and burned Basra and slaughtered its people. Their marsh state endured fourteen years, until the regent al-Muwaffaq stormed their capital, al-Mukhtara, in 883.
    • Ingolf Settles IcelandA.D. 874, Reykjavik, Iceland. Ingolf Arnarson, a Norwegian chieftain, cast the pillars of his high seat overboard as he neared Iceland and vowed to settle wherever they came ashore. His thralls found them at a bay he named Reykjavik, the bay of smoke, for the steam of its hot springs. Within sixty years, the Book of Settlements records, every habitable shore had been taken by Norse families and their Irish slaves.
    • Alfred Victorious at EdingtonA.D. 878, Edington, England. Surprised at Chippenham in midwinter by Guthrum's Danes, Alfred of Wessex took refuge in the marshes of Athelney. In May 878 he gathered the men of Somerset, Wiltshire, and Hampshire and routed the Danes at Edington. Guthrum accepted baptism with Alfred as his godfather, and England was soon divided between Wessex and the Danelaw.
    • Huang Chao Takes Chang'anA.D. 881, Chang'an, Shaanxi, China. Huang Chao, a salt smuggler who had failed the examinations, led a rebel host the length of China, massacring the foreign merchants of Canton in 879, and entered Chang'an in triumph. He proclaimed himself emperor of Qi, but his men pillaged the city and slaughtered its officials. The Tang outlived his defeat only as a shell, ruled by its generals.
