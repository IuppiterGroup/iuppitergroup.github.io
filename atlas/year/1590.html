    The chronicle of these years

    • The Honnō-ji IncidentA.D. 1582, Heian-kyō, Kyoto, Japan. Oda Nobunaga, who had seized Kyoto, burned the monastery fortress of Mount Hiei, and broken the Takeda horsemen with massed musketry at Nagashino, lay at the temple of Honnō-ji with a small guard when his general Akechi Mitsuhide turned on him. Nobunaga died in the burning temple. Eleven days later his lieutenant Toyotomi Hideyoshi destroyed Mitsuhide at Yamazaki.
    • The Spanish ArmadaA.D. 1588, Gravelines, Flanders. Philip II sent some 130 ships under the Duke of Medina Sidonia up the Channel to carry Parma's army to England. Driven from their anchorage at Calais by fireships on the night of 7 August 1588, the Spaniards were mauled off Gravelines by Howard and Drake. Storms wrecked dozens more on the coasts of Scotland and Ireland, and barely half came home.
    • TondibiA.D. 1591, Tondibi, Mali. Sultan Ahmad al-Mansur of Morocco, coveting the gold of the Sudan, sent some four thousand men, many of them Spanish renegades and Andalusian exiles armed with arquebuses, across the Sahara under the eunuch Judar Pasha. At Tondibi in March 1591 their gunfire scattered the far larger host of Askia Ishaq II; Gao and Timbuktu fell, and the Songhai empire, last of the three great empires of the western Sahel, broke apart.
    • Admiral Yi at Hansan IslandA.D. 1592, Hansando, Korea. Toyotomi Hideyoshi, master of all Japan, sent some 150,000 men into Korea on the road, he boasted, to the conquest of Ming China; they took Busan and Hanseong within three weeks. At sea it went otherwise: Admiral Yi Sun-sin lured the Japanese fleet out of the Gyeonnaeryang strait off Hansan Island and destroyed it in a crane-wing formation, cutting the invaders' supply by sea.
    • Naresuan's Elephant DuelA.D. 1593, Nong Sarai, Thailand. Naresuan, the Siamese prince who had grown up a hostage at Pegu and in 1584 renounced allegiance to Burma, met the Burmese crown prince Mingyi Swa at Nong Sarai near Suphan Buri. The two princes fought from the backs of war elephants, and Naresuan cut down his rival with his blade. Siam was free, and Naresuan carried the war into Burma and Cambodia.
    • The Dutch Arrive at BantenA.D. 1596, Banten, Java. Cornelis de Houtman's four ships, after a voyage on which scurvy and fever carried off many of their men, reached the pepper port of Banten in June 1596. Houtman quarreled with the Bantenese and bombarded towns along the coast of Java, and fewer than ninety of his 249 men came home; yet the profit set off a rush of Dutch companies, merged in 1602 into the East India Company.
    • The Edict of NantesA.D. 1598, Nantes, Brittany. Henry IV, the Huguenot king who had turned Catholic to win Paris, ended thirty-six years of religious war with the edict signed at Nantes in April 1598. The Huguenots received freedom of conscience, public worship in named places, equal right to offices, and some hundred fortified towns as surety. The settlement held for nearly ninety years.
    • Shah Abbas Makes Isfahan His CapitalA.D. 1598, Isfahan, Persia. Abbas I, who had won back Khorasan from the Uzbeks and remade his army around a corps of slave soldiers taken from Georgia and Armenia, moved his capital to Isfahan in 1598. Around a vast square, the Naqsh-e Jahan, he raised the royal mosque, the mosque of Shaykh Lutfallah, the Ali Qapu palace, and the royal bazaar. Persians said that Isfahan was half the world.
