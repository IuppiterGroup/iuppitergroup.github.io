    The chronicle of these years

    • Romulus Founds Rome753 B.C., The Palatine, Rome. By tradition, on the twenty-first of April, Romulus plows the sacred furrow around the Palatine and kills his brother Remus for leaping the new wall: "So perish whoever else shall leap over my walls" (Livy 1.7). The Romans will count their years from this day, ab urbe condita.
    • Corinth Founds Syracusec. 733 B.C., Syracuse, Sicily. Corinthian settlers under Archias landed on the island of Ortygia, with its spring of Arethusa and its fine harbor, and founded Syracuse, which grew into the greatest Greek city of the west. It was one of a wave of colonies, Naxos, Leontini, Megara Hyblaea, and others, that made Sicily and southern Italy a new Greece.
    • Piye Conquers Egyptc. 725 B.C., Memphis, Egypt. Piye, king of Kush, ruling from Napata beneath the holy mountain of Jebel Barkal, came north to save Egypt from the Libyan chiefs who had divided it. He took Hermopolis, stormed Memphis, and received the submission of the Delta princes, chiding King Nimlot on his victory stela for letting his horses go hungry. His Twenty-fifth Dynasty ruled Egypt for some sixty years.
    • The Fall of Samariac. 722 B.C., Samaria, Israel. Hoshea of Israel withheld his tribute and sought help from Egypt, and Shalmaneser V laid siege to Samaria for three years. The city fell, and Sargon II, by his own boast, deported some twenty-seven thousand of its people to Assyria and Media and settled foreigners in their place. The northern kingdom was no more, and its ten tribes passed out of history.
    • Sennacherib Before Lachish and Jerusalem701 B.C., Lachish, Judah. When Hezekiah of Judah rebelled, Sennacherib took forty-six of his walled towns and stormed Lachish, a siege his sculptors carved on the walls of his palace at Nineveh. Hezekiah, shut up 'like a bird in a cage' in Jerusalem, paid a heavy tribute, but the city was not taken, and the Bible tells how the angel of the Lord struck the Assyrian camp.
    • The Sages of the Upanishadsc. 700 B.C., Janakpur, Nepal. At the court of Janaka, king of Videha, the sage Yajnavalkya disputed with the Brahmins and with the learned woman Gargi on the nature of the self. Their teachings, set down in the Brihadaranyaka Upanishad, held that the inmost self, atman, is one with brahman, the ground of all being. On this rock rose the philosophy of later Hinduism.
