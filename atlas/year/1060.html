    The chronicle of these years

    • The Great SchismA.D. 1054, Hagia Sophia, Constantinople. On 16 July 1054 Cardinal Humbert, legate of Pope Leo IX, strode into Hagia Sophia at the hour of the liturgy and laid on the altar a bull excommunicating Patriarch Michael Cerularius. The patriarch answered with anathemas of his own. Quarrels over the authority of Rome, the Filioque, and unleavened bread hardened into a rupture between the Latin and Greek Churches that has never healed.
    • Tughril Beg Enters BaghdadA.D. 1055, Baghdad, Iraq. Invited by the caliph al-Qa'im to deliver him from the Buyids, the Seljuk Tughril Beg entered Baghdad in December 1055 and was hailed as sultan; the caliph later styled him King of East and West. The last Buyid amir was sent into captivity. The Seljuks, fierce champions of the Sunna, took up the sword of the caliphate, and the age of the Turkish sultans in the heartlands of Islam began.
    • The Banu Hilal Sack KairouanA.D. 1057, Kairouan, Ifriqiya. When the Zirid emir al-Mu'izz broke with Cairo, the Fatimids loosed upon Ifriqiya the Bedouin tribes of the Banu Hilal and Banu Sulaym, long camped in Upper Egypt. They overran the countryside like locusts, and in 1057 Kairouan was abandoned and plundered. Ibn Khaldun wrote that wherever these Arabs conquered, ruin followed; the Maghreb took their speech for good.
    • Anawrahta Conquers ThatonA.D. 1057, Thaton, Burma. Urged by the Mon monk Shin Arahan to seek the pure scriptures, Anawrahta marched on the Mon city of Thaton when its king Manuha refused to share them. After a long siege he carried Manuha, his court, his artisans, and the Pali scriptures back to Pagan. Theravada Buddhism became the faith of the Burmans, and Mon art and letters shaped their kingdom.
    • HastingsA.D. 1066, Sussex, England. William, Duke of Normandy, breaks the Saxon shield wall at Hastings after a long day of slaughter. King Harold falls, by legend with an arrow in his eye. On Christmas Day William is crowned at Westminster, and the lords of England will speak French for three centuries.
    • Harald Hardrada at Stamford BridgeA.D. 1066, Stamford Bridge, England. Harald Hardrada of Norway, the most famous warrior of the North and once a captain of the Varangian Guard, landed in Yorkshire with Tostig, the English king's brother. On 25 September 1066 Harold Godwinson, marching north from London in four days, fell on them at Stamford Bridge and slew both. Three days later William of Normandy landed in Sussex.
    • Al-Bakri Describes GhanaA.D. 1068, Koumbi Saleh, Mauritania. Writing at Cordoba from the reports of merchants, the geographer al-Bakri described the capital of Ghana as two towns six miles apart, one of stone for the Muslim traders with twelve mosques, the other for the king, Tunka Manin, who gave audience in a domed pavilion among horses caparisoned in gold and could put 200,000 warriors in the field. His wealth rested on gold from the south and salt from the desert.
    • The Battle of ManzikertA.D. 1071, Manzikert, Armenia. On 26 August 1071 the Seljuk sultan Alp Arslan met Emperor Romanos IV Diogenes near Manzikert, north of Lake Van. Feigned flight drew the Roman host apart, the rearguard under Andronikos Doukas deserted, and the emperor was taken prisoner, the first to fall into a Muslim ruler's hands. Alp Arslan released him on easy terms, but Turkmen bands poured into Anatolia, and Asia Minor began its long change into Turkey.
