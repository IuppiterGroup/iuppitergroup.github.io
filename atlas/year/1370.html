    The chronicle of these years

    • The Golden Bull of Charles IVA.D. 1356, Nuremberg, Germany. At diets in Nuremberg and Metz, Charles IV issued a law sealed in gold that fixed the election of the King of the Romans in seven electors: the archbishops of Mainz, Cologne, and Trier, the King of Bohemia, the Count Palatine, the Duke of Saxony, and the Margrave of Brandenburg. The pope's approval went unmentioned. The bull governed the Empire until 1806.
    • Zhu Yuanzhang Founds the MingA.D. 1368, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China. Zhu Yuanzhang, an orphan of famine who had begged as a monk and risen through the Red Turban armies, proclaimed the Ming, the Brilliant dynasty, at Nanjing. That summer his general Xu Da marched north and took Dadu without a battle, and the last Yuan emperor, Toghon Temür, fled to the steppe. China was ruled by a Chinese house once more.
    • Timur Master of TransoxianaA.D. 1370, Balkh, Khorasan. Timur, a petty noble of the Barlas tribe lamed by arrows in his youth, besieged his former ally and brother-in-law Amir Husayn in Balkh, and on Husayn's death in April 1370 was proclaimed sovereign of the Chagatai lands. Not being of Genghis Khan's blood, he ruled in the name of puppet khans, taking only the styles of emir and royal son-in-law.
    • Ibn Khaldun Writes the MuqaddimahA.D. 1377, Qal'at Ibn Salama, Algeria. Weary of the courts of North Africa and Granada, the Tunisian scholar and statesman Ibn Khaldun withdrew to the castle of Ibn Salama in the hills of western Algeria, and there in 1377 completed the Muqaddimah, the introduction to his universal history. Its doctrine of asabiyya, the group feeling by which desert peoples conquer cities and are in turn corrupted by them, founded the philosophy of history.
    • The Great Western SchismA.D. 1378, Rome, Italy. In 1378 the cardinals, under the threats of a Roman mob demanding an Italian pope, elected Urban VI; repelled by his violence, most of them withdrew to Fondi and chose Clement VII, who returned to Avignon. For nearly forty years Christendom had two popes, after 1409 three, until the Council of Constance deposed or accepted the resignation of all and elected Martin V in 1417.
    • Dmitry Donskoy at KulikovoA.D. 1380, Kulikovo Field, Russia. On 8 September 1380 Grand Prince Dmitry of Moscow, blessed by St. Sergius of Radonezh, led the princes of Rus' across the Don against Mamai, warlord of the Golden Horde. After a day of slaughter the Tatars broke. Two years later Tokhtamysh burned Moscow, but the spell of Tatar invincibility was broken, and Dmitry was called Donskoy, of the Don.
    • The Peasants' RevoltA.D. 1381, Smithfield, London. Provoked by a third poll tax, the commons of Essex and Kent rose under Wat Tyler and the priest John Ball, who asked, 'When Adam delved and Eve span, who was then the gentleman?' They burned the Savoy palace and beheaded the Archbishop of Canterbury. At Smithfield on 15 June 1381 the mayor struck Tyler down, and the boy king Richard II rode forward to lead the leaderless rebels away.
    • The Union of KrewoA.D. 1385, Krewo, Lithuania. By the act sealed at Krewo on 14 August 1385, Jogaila, pagan grand duke of Lithuania, pledged to accept baptism with his people, join his lands to Poland, and marry the young Queen Jadwiga. Crowned at Kraków in 1386 as Władysław II, he founded the Jagiellonian dynasty, and the last pagan state of Europe entered Latin Christendom.
