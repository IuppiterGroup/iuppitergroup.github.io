    The chronicle of these years

    • The Second Battle of TarainA.D. 1192, Taraori, Haryana, India. A year later Mu'izz al-Din returned with some 120,000 horsemen. Feigning retreat through the day and wheeling his mounted archers in relays against the Rajput host, he broke it at dusk with a charge of twelve thousand picked horsemen. Prithviraj was taken in flight and put to death. The road to Delhi lay open, and Muslim rule over northern India began.
    • The Monasteries of Bihar Destroyedc. A.D. 1200, Bihar Sharif, Bihar, India. Bakhtiyar Khalji, a Turkish adventurer, rode into Bihar and stormed the walled monastery of Odantapuri, which he took for a fortress. Minhaj records that its inmates, shaven-headed men he took for Brahmins, were all slain, and that none was left alive who could read its books. Nalanda and Vikramashila were laid waste in the same years, and Buddhism all but vanished from the land of its birth.
    • The Rock Churches of Lalibelac. A.D. 1200, Lalibela, Ethiopia. Gebre Meskel Lalibela, king of the Zagwe dynasty, had eleven churches hewn downward into the living red rock of Roha, among them the cross-shaped Bete Giyorgis. Ethiopian tradition holds that he meant to raise a New Jerusalem in the highlands after Saladin's capture of the Holy City in 1187 made the pilgrimage perilous; the town has borne his name ever since.
    • The Crusaders Sack ConstantinopleA.D. 1204, Constantinople, Thrace. Diverted to Constantinople to restore a pretender, the Fourth Crusade quarreled with the Greeks and on 12 April 1204 stormed the city from the Golden Horn. For three days knights and Venetians pillaged the treasures of nine centuries, stripping churches and melting statues. A Latin empire was set up, and the Roman East was broken into fragments that never wholly reunited.
    • Bakhtiyar Khalji Takes NadiaA.D. 1204, Nabadwip, West Bengal, India. Riding ahead of his army with only eighteen horsemen, so Minhaj tells, Bakhtiyar Khalji passed the gates of Nadia, taken by the guards for horse dealers, and burst upon the palace of the aged Sena king Lakshmana Sena as he sat at his meal. The king fled barefoot by a back door to eastern Bengal, and the Turks made Lakhnauti their capital.
    • Genghis KhanA.D. 1206, The Onon River. At a great assembly by the Onon River, the tribes of the steppe raise up Temujin as Genghis Khan. He has united the Mongols by sword and by oath. Within a lifetime his sons and grandsons will build the largest contiguous land empire the world has ever seen, from Korea to the plains of Russia.
    • Aibak, the Slave SultanA.D. 1206, Lahore, Punjab, Pakistan. When Mu'izz al-Din was murdered on the Jhelum in 1206, his Turkish slave-general Qutb al-Din Aibak took power over the Indian conquests at Lahore. Thus began the Delhi Sultanate, ruled at first by former slaves. Aibak had begun the Quwwat ul-Islam mosque at Delhi from the pillars of demolished temples and the Qutb Minar beside it, but died in 1210 when his horse fell on him at polo.
    • Magna CartaA.D. 1215, Runnymede, England. In the meadow at Runnymede beside the Thames, King John, beaten in France and confronted by rebel barons who held London, set his seal to the Great Charter in June 1215. Annulled within weeks by the pope, it was reissued under his son, and its promise that no free man be imprisoned or dispossessed save by the lawful judgment of his peers or the law of the land became a cornerstone of English liberty.
