    The chronicle of these years

    • Zhu Wen Ends the TangA.D. 907, Kaifeng, Henan, China. Zhu Wen, once a lieutenant of Huang Chao and now the strongest general in the north, forced the last Tang boy emperor to abdicate and proclaimed his own Later Liang dynasty at Kaifeng. He had already razed Chang'an and murdered the Tang court officials. China broke into the Five Dynasties of the north and the Ten Kingdoms of the south.
    • The Fatimid Mahdi ProclaimedA.D. 909, Raqqada, Ifriqiya. The Isma'ili missionary Abu Abdallah al-Shi'i, preaching among the Kutama Berbers, overthrew the Aghlabids and drove the last emir from Raqqada in 909. He fetched his hidden master Ubayd Allah from Sijilmasa, and in January 910 proclaimed him caliph with the name al-Mahdi. A Shi'ite caliphate, descended by its own reckoning from Fatima, now challenged Baghdad for the rule of Islam.
    • The Last Long Count DateA.D. 909, Toniná, Mexico. At Toniná in the Chiapas highlands, a small monument carved in January 909 bears the last known date in the Maya Long Count. Through the ninth century the great cities of the southern lowlands, Copán, Palenque, Tikal, and Calakmul, had ceased one by one to raise monuments and emptied of people, worn down by war, drought, and the exhaustion of their land.
    • The Founding of ClunyA.D. 910, Cluny, Burgundy. Duke William of Aquitaine gave his hunting estate at Cluny to Abbot Berno and his monks and placed the house under the protection of the apostles Peter and Paul, free of any lord or bishop. Under its great abbots Cluny led a reform of monastic life across Europe; by the twelfth century it ruled hundreds of priories, and its abbey church was the largest in Christendom.
    • Rollo Receives NormandyA.D. 911, Saint-Clair-sur-Epte, France. At Saint-Clair-sur-Epte, by tradition, Charles the Simple granted the Viking chief Rollo the lands about Rouen on condition that he defend the Seine against other Northmen and receive baptism. Rollo's Norsemen became Normans, and within a century and a half his descendants would conquer England and southern Italy.
    • Abaoji Takes the Imperial TitleA.D. 916, Shangjing, Inner Mongolia, China. Yelü Abaoji, who had made himself khagan of the eight Khitan tribes by setting aside their custom of electing a new chief every three years, proclaimed himself emperor in the Chinese manner and soon built a walled Supreme Capital on the steppe. His state, named Liao in 947, ruled nomads by tribal custom and farmers by Chinese law, and gave the West its old name for China, Cathay.
    • Simeon Triumphs at AchelousA.D. 917, Achelous, Bulgaria. Simeon of Bulgaria, schooled at Constantinople and called half-Greek, sought the imperial crown itself. On 20 August 917 his army fell on the Byzantine host of Leo Phokas by the river Achelous near Anchialos and destroyed it in one of the bloodiest battles of the age. For a decade Simeon was master of the Balkans, styling himself emperor of the Bulgarians and the Romans.
    • Wang Geon Founds GoryeoA.D. 918, Kaesong, Korea. The officers of the tyrant Gung Ye, who called himself a living Maitreya and had murdered his own wife and sons, overthrew him and raised the general Wang Geon as king. Wang named his kingdom Goryeo after old Goguryeo, the name from which Korea comes, and set his capital at his home of Songak, later Kaesong. In 935 Silla yielded to him, and in 936 he united the peninsula.
