    The chronicle of these years

    • The Black Banners of KhorasanA.D. 747, Merv, Khorasan. In the villages around Merv a freedman of obscure origin named Abu Muslim unfurled the black banners in June 747, calling for a caliph from the family of the Prophet. Persian converts, long held second-class by their Arab masters, and discontented Arab settlers alike flocked to him. Within a year Merv was his, and his armies were moving west upon Iraq.
    • The End of the UmayyadsA.D. 750, The Great Zab, Mesopotamia. On the Great Zab the last Umayyad caliph, Marwan II, was routed by the Abbasid army in January 750. He fled to Egypt and was slain there. Abu al-Abbas, called al-Saffah, the shedder of blood, was proclaimed caliph at Kufa, and at a banquet near Jaffa his uncle Abdallah had some eighty princes of the fallen house clubbed to death. One youth escaped to Spain.
    • Gopala Elected King of Bengalc. A.D. 750, Varendra, Bangladesh. After a century of anarchy that the Bengalis called the law of the fishes, when the strong devoured the weak, the chiefs of Bengal chose a man named Gopala as their king. His son Dharmapala recorded the election on a copper plate. The Pala dynasty he founded ruled Bengal and Bihar for four centuries and became the last great royal patrons of Buddhism in India.
    • Pepin the Short Made KingA.D. 751, Soissons, France. Pope Zacharias having ruled that he who held the power should bear the title, Pepin the Short had the last Merovingian, Childeric III, shorn and sent to a monastery. The Franks chose Pepin king at Soissons in November 751, and the bishops anointed him with holy oil, the first such anointing of a Frankish king. The Carolingian dynasty had begun.
    • The Battle of TalasA.D. 751, Talas River, Central Asia. Near the Talas river, Ziyad ibn Salih, general of the new Abbasid caliph, met a Tang Chinese army under the Korean-born Gao Xianzhi in July 751. The defection of the Karluk Turks in mid-battle turned the day for the Muslims. China never again pushed west of the Tian Shan, and Transoxiana remained within Islam. Tradition holds that captured Chinese craftsmen taught papermaking at Samarkand.
    • The Great Buddha of Tōdai-jiA.D. 752, Nara, Japan. Emperor Shōmu, hoping to shield the realm from plague and rebellion, had a bronze Buddha of Vairocana some fifteen metres high cast at Tōdai-ji, using most of the copper in Japan. At the eye-opening ceremony the Indian monk Bodhisena painted in the pupils before priests from across Asia. The hall, rebuilt, is still among the largest wooden buildings in the world.
    • Boniface Martyred at DokkumA.D. 754, Dokkum, Frisia. Winfrid of Wessex, called Boniface, had felled the oak of the thunder god at Geismar, organized the Church of Germany, and set bishops over sees from Salzburg to Würzburg. Nearly eighty, he went back to preach among the Frisians, and on 5 June 754 a band of heathen fell on his camp at Dokkum and slew him with his companions. Germans honor him as their apostle.
    • The Rebellion of An LushanA.D. 755, Fanyang, Hebei, China. An Lushan, a general of Sogdian and Turkic blood who commanded three frontier armies, rose at Fanyang, took Luoyang, and proclaimed himself emperor of Yan. Emperor Xuanzong fled Chang'an, and his mutinous guards forced him to have his beloved Yang Guifei strangled at Mawei. Eight years of war followed, and the Tang census fell from over fifty million to under seventeen.
