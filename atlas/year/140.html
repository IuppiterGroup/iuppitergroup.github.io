    The chronicle of these years

    • The Empire at Its HeightA.D. 117, Selinus, Cilicia. Trajan, having added Dacia and pressed his legions to the Persian Gulf, dies on the road home at Selinus in Cilicia. The Empire now reaches from Britain to Mesopotamia, the widest the Roman eagles will ever fly.
    • Hadrian's WallA.D. 122, Housesteads, Britain. Hadrian, who spent half his reign touring the frontiers, came to Britain and ordered a wall built from the Tyne to the Solway, eighty Roman miles, to separate the Romans from the barbarians. Of stone and turf, with milecastles, turrets, and forts like Housesteads, it marked the limit of the empire for nearly three centuries.
    • Gautamiputra Satakarni Destroys the Kshaharatasc. A.D. 124, Nashik, Maharashtra, India. Gautamiputra Satakarni, king of the Satavahanas of the Deccan, overthrew the Saka satrap Nahapana and restored his house's power from the Narmada to the Krishna. In the Jogalthembi hoard thousands of Nahapana's silver coins were found restruck with Gautamiputra's name. His mother Gautami Balashri praised him in a cave inscription at Nashik as destroyer of Sakas, Yavanas, and Pahlavas.
    • Kanishka the Greatc. A.D. 127, Purushapura (Peshawar), Pakistan. Kanishka, greatest of the Kushan emperors, ruled from the Oxus to the Ganges with his winter capital at Purushapura. His Rabatak inscription tells that he issued his edicts in the Bactrian tongue and that his rule reached Pataliputra and Champa. Buddhists remember him as a second Ashoka: he raised a towering stupa at Purushapura, and his coins bear one of the first images of the Buddha.
    • The Fall of BetarA.D. 135, Betar, Judea. Hadrian's plan to refound Jerusalem as the Roman colony of Aelia Capitolina provoked a second Jewish revolt under Simon bar Kokhba, hailed by Rabbi Akiva as the star out of Jacob. After three years the last stronghold, Betar, fell; Dio says more than half a million died, and Jews were forbidden to enter Jerusalem.
    • The Antonine PlagueA.D. 165, Seleucia on the Tigris, Mesopotamia. In the Parthian war of Lucius Verus, Avidius Cassius sacked Seleucia on the Tigris, and the legions carried home a pestilence, probably smallpox, that spread through the empire for twenty years. The physician Galen described its fevers and black pustules; it may have killed a tenth of the people of the empire.
