    The chronicle of these years

    • Austronesian Farmers Reach Luzonc. 2200 B.C., Cagayan Valley, Philippines. Seafaring farmers from Taiwan crossed the Luzon Strait and settled the valley of the Cagayan, bringing red-slipped pottery, polished stone adzes, and the Austronesian tongue. From these first villages, at sites such as Nagsabaran, their descendants spread through the Philippines and Indonesia, and at last across the Pacific and the Indian Ocean as far as Easter Island and Madagascar.
    • Akkad Falls to the Gutiansc. 2193 B.C., Akkad, Mesopotamia. After the death of Shar-kali-sharri the empire of Sargon dissolved. The Sumerian King List asks, 'Who was king? Who was not king?' The Gutians came down from the Zagros, the cities of Sumer went their own way, and the fall of Akkad passed into legend as the curse of the gods upon Naram-Sin's house.
    • The Old Kingdom Falls Apartc. 2180 B.C., Memphis, Egypt. Pepi II, who came to the throne as a child and was said to have reigned for some ninety years, outlived his heirs, and after him the power of Memphis crumbled. Provincial governors ruled as princes, low Nile floods brought famine, and for more than a century rival kings at Herakleopolis and Thebes contended for the Two Lands.
    • Ur-Nammu Founds the Third Dynasty of Urc. 2112 B.C., Ur, Sumer. Ur-Nammu, once governor of Ur, made himself king of Sumer and Akkad, raised the great ziggurat of the moon god Nanna, and issued the oldest law code yet found, setting fines in silver for injuries. His son Shulgi ruled for forty-eight years over a state whose scribes counted every sheep, every measure of barley, and every day of labor.
    • The Laws of Ur-Nammuc. 2100 B.C., Ur, Sumer. The oldest law code to survive was issued at Ur by Ur-Nammu or his son Shulgi. Its prologue promises that the orphan will not be given to the rich nor the widow to the powerful, and its laws set fines in silver for injuries where later codes would demand an eye for an eye.
    • Yu the Great Founds the Xiac. 2070 B.C., Yangcheng, China. The Chinese histories tell that Yu the Great tamed the floods of the Yellow River by digging channels to the sea, labouring thirteen years without entering his own gate, and that the throne passed to his son, founding the Xia, the first dynasty in which rule passed from father to son.
    • Mentuhotep II Reunites Egyptc. 2040 B.C., Deir el-Bahari, Thebes, Egypt. Mentuhotep II, prince of Thebes, overcame the kings of Herakleopolis and joined the Two Lands again, opening the Middle Kingdom. He built his terraced mortuary temple in the bay of cliffs at Deir el-Bahari, and in a tomb nearby were found some sixty of his soldiers, slain in battle and buried with honor.
    • The Elamites Sack Urc. 2004 B.C., Ur, Sumer. Weakened by famine and by Amorite tribes pressing in from the west, the empire of Ur fell to an army from Elam and Shimashki, which stormed the city and led King Ibbi-Sin away in chains to Anshan. The Lament for Ur mourned the dead lying in the streets and the abandoned temple of Nanna.
