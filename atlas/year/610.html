    The chronicle of these years

    • Phocas Overthrows MauriceA.D. 602, Chalcedon, Bithynia. Ordered to winter beyond the Danube among the Slavs, the army mutinied and raised the centurion Phocas on a shield. Maurice fled the capital, was taken, and at Chalcedon watched his five sons butchered before his own head fell. Khosrow II of Persia, whom Maurice had restored to his throne, took the murder as cause for a war that lasted twenty-six years.
    • The Grand CanalA.D. 605, Yangzhou, Jiangsu, China. Emperor Yang of Sui set more than a million labourers to dig the Tongji Canal from Luoyang to the Huai, and then the Yongji Canal to the north, joining them with older works into a waterway from the Yangtze to the frontier near Beijing. Grain from the rich south could now feed the capitals and armies of the north, and the emperor sailed to Jiangdu in dragon boats.
    • Harsha Ascends at ThanesarA.D. 606, Thanesar, Haryana, India. When his elder brother Rajyavardhana was treacherously slain by Shashanka of Gauda, the sixteen-year-old Harsha took the throne of Thanesar and vowed to rid the earth of his enemies. He rescued his widowed sister Rajyashri as she was about to mount the pyre, made Kannauj his capital, and built the last great native empire of northern India. His era begins in 606.
    • The First Revelation on Mount Hirac. A.D. 610, Jabal al-Nour, Arabia. In a cave on Mount Hira above Mecca, Muhammad ibn Abdallah, a merchant of the Quraysh some forty years of age, began to receive the revelations that became the Quran, opening with the command "Recite in the name of thy Lord." For a dozen years he preached the one God in Mecca, gathering a small following that the city's chiefs mocked and persecuted.
    • The Battle of the SalsuA.D. 612, Chongchon River, Korea. Emperor Yang of Sui led one of the largest armies of its age, over a million men, against Goguryeo. A picked force of three hundred thousand pushed toward Pyongyang, but the general Eulji Mundeok lured it on, starved it, and fell upon it as it crossed the Salsu River; only 2,700 men are said to have returned. The Sui never recovered.
    • Pakal the Great of PalenqueA.D. 615, Palenque, Mexico. K'inich Janaab Pakal took the throne of Palenque at the age of twelve and reigned for sixty-eight years, raising a city of graceful palaces and temples on a shelf above the lowland plain. In 1952 the archaeologist Alberto Ruz found his tomb deep beneath the Temple of the Inscriptions, the king laid in a jade mask under a sarcophagus lid carved with his descent into the underworld.
    • Ishanavarman's City of Brickc. A.D. 616, Sambor Prei Kuk, Cambodia. Ishanavarman I, king of Chenla, ruled from Ishanapura on the Stung Sen, where more than a hundred brick towers rose within moated enclosures, some carved with the flying palaces of the gods. The Chinese knew him as lord of thirty walled towns of several thousand households each; his city, now Sambor Prei Kuk, was the greatest of Cambodia before Angkor.
    • The Hijra to MedinaA.D. 622, Medina, Arabia. Warned of a plot against his life, Muhammad slipped out of Mecca with Abu Bakr and reached the oasis of Yathrib, thereafter Medina, the City of the Prophet, in September 622. There he became lawgiver, judge, and war leader of a community bound by faith rather than blood. The Muslim calendar counts its years from this emigration.
