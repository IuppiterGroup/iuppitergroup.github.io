    The chronicle of these years

    • The Durand LineA.D. 1893, Kabul, Afghanistan. Sir Mortimer Durand, foreign secretary of British India, agreed with the Amir Abdur Rahman Khan at Kabul on a boundary between Afghanistan and the Raj running some 2,600 kilometres through the Pashtun and Baluch hills. It split the Pashtun tribes between two states, and no Afghan government has since accepted it as the border with Pakistan.
    • French Gunboats at PaknamA.D. 1893, Paknam, Siam. Pressing claims to the lands east of the Mekong, France sent two gunboats past the forts at Paknam at the river mouth on 13 July 1893, and they anchored before the Grand Palace. Facing blockade, King Chulalongkorn yielded all Laos east of the Mekong. Siam survived as a buffer between British Burma and French Indochina, but at the price of its outer provinces.
    • Votes for Women in New ZealandA.D. 1893, Wellington, New Zealand. After petitions bearing some thirty-two thousand signatures, gathered by Kate Sheppard and the Women's Christian Temperance Union, the Electoral Act became law on 19 September 1893. New Zealand thus became the first self-governing country in the world to grant women the vote, and that November nearly two-thirds of its adult women went to the polls.
    • The Hamidian MassacresA.D. 1895, Diyarbakır, Anatolia. After the Armenians of Sasun resisted Kurdish tribute-takers in 1894 and were butchered by troops and irregulars, a wave of massacres swept the Armenian towns of Anatolia in 1895 and 1896, at Trebizond, Erzurum, Diyarbakır, Urfa, and Constantinople itself, under the rule of Abdul Hamid II. At least a hundred thousand Armenians perished; in the cathedral of Urfa some three thousand were burned alive.
    • The Treaty of ShimonosekiA.D. 1895, Shimonoseki, Japan. Japan's modern army and navy had shattered the Qing forces in Korea and at sea off the Yalu, and taken Port Arthur and Weihaiwei. At Shimonoseki Li Hongzhang, wounded by a Japanese assassin during the talks, signed away Taiwan and the Penghu islands, recognized the independence of Korea, and agreed to an indemnity of two hundred million taels.
    • The Cry of the KatipunanA.D. 1896, Caloocan, Philippines. Andres Bonifacio, a warehouse clerk, had founded the secret Katipunan to win independence by arms. Discovered by the Spanish, its members tore up their cedulas, the tax certificates of Spanish rule, in the hills north of Manila in late August 1896 and rose in revolt. In Cavite the young Emilio Aguinaldo won the first victories and soon eclipsed Bonifacio, whom his rivals put to death in 1897.
    • The Execution of Jose RizalA.D. 1896, Bagumbayan, Manila. Jose Rizal, physician, poet, and author of the novels Noli Me Tangere and El Filibusterismo, which laid bare the abuses of friars and officials, had never joined the revolution. The Spanish nonetheless tried him for rebellion and shot him at Bagumbayan field on 30 December 1896. His farewell poem, written in his cell, made him the martyr of the Filipino nation.
    • AdwaA.D. 1896, Adwa, Ethiopia. When Italy claimed, under a disputed clause of the Treaty of Wuchale, a protectorate over Ethiopia, Emperor Menelik II summoned the lords of the empire, and the Empress Taytu marched beside him. On 1 March 1896 his host of some 100,000 crushed General Baratieri's army of 17,000 among the mountains of Adwa, killing some 7,000; Italy recognized Ethiopia's independence, and it alone of the old African kingdoms escaped the partition.
