    The chronicle of these years

    • The Battle of the YarmoukA.D. 636, Yarmouk River, Syria. In August 636 the great army Heraclius had gathered to recover Syria met the Arabs under Khalid ibn al-Walid and Abu Ubayda in the gorges of the Yarmouk. After six days of battle the imperial host was broken and driven into the ravines. Heraclius left Syria, by tradition with the words "Farewell, Syria, my fair province." Syria was lost to the empire.
    • Alexandria Yields to the ArabsA.D. 642, Alexandria, Egypt. By a treaty made with the Patriarch Cyrus, the Roman garrison sailed away from Alexandria in September 642, and the second city of the empire, founded by Alexander nearly a thousand years before, passed to the Arabs. Egypt, the granary of Constantinople, now fed Medina. The Coptic Christians, long at odds with the imperial church, paid the poll tax to new masters.
    • Xuanzang at the Assembly of KannaujA.D. 643, Kannauj, Uttar Pradesh, India. The Chinese pilgrim Xuanzang, who had crossed the deserts and the Hindu Kush and studied for years at Nalanda, was summoned by Harsha to a great assembly at Kannauj. Before the kings of eighteen realms and thousands of monks and Brahmins he expounded the Mahayana for eighteen days, and none could answer him. He carried 657 books of scripture home to Chang'an.
    • The Fall of the SogaA.D. 645, Asuka, Nara, Japan. Prince Naka no Ōe and Nakatomi no Kamatari cut down Soga no Iruka before the empress in the Itabuki palace, and his father Emishi burned himself in his mansion. The Soga ascendancy ended, and the Taika reforms that followed declared all land and people subject to the throne and began to remake Japan in the image of the Tang state.
    • The Last of the SasaniansA.D. 651, Merv, Khorasan. A fugitive in his own empire, Yazdegerd III, King of Kings, came at last to Merv, whose governor would not stand by him. He was murdered there by a miller, says the tale, for the sake of his rich garments, and with him ended the dynasty of Ardashir after four centuries. Arab garrisons at Merv soon looked out across the Oxus toward Transoxiana.
    • The Nubian Archers Hold DongolaA.D. 652, Old Dongola, Sudan. When the Arab conquerors of Egypt marched up the Nile under Abdallah ibn Sa'd, the bowmen of Christian Makuria, whom the Arabs called 'the pupil-smiters,' met them before Dongola. A catapult struck the cathedral, but the Nubians held, and the Baqt treaty that followed, an annual tribute of 360 slaves exchanged for grain, wine, and cloth, kept Christian Nubia free for six centuries.
    • The Murder of UthmanA.D. 656, Medina, Arabia. Rebels from Egypt and Iraq, aggrieved by the favor the aged caliph showed his Umayyad kinsmen, besieged Uthman ibn Affan in his house at Medina and cut him down as he read the Quran, his blood falling upon its pages. Ali ibn Abi Talib was acclaimed caliph in the confusion, and the first civil war of Islam, the fitna, began.
    • The Arbitration of SiffinA.D. 657, Siffin, Syria. On the plain of Siffin by the Euphrates, Ali faced Mu'awiya, governor of Syria and avenger of his kinsman Uthman. When the Syrians were near defeat they raised leaves of the Quran upon their lances and called for arbitration. Ali consented, and a part of his army, crying that judgment belonged to God alone, departed from him. These were the Kharijites, the first sect of Islam.
