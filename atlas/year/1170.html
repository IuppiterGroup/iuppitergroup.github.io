    The chronicle of these years

    • Henry II Lands in IrelandA.D. 1171, Waterford, Ireland. Cambro-Norman knights called in by Diarmait Mac Murchada, the exiled king of Leinster, had taken Wexford, Waterford, and Dublin, and their leader Strongbow had married Diarmait's daughter. Lest they found a rival kingdom, Henry II landed at Waterford in October 1171 with a great army and received the submission of most of the Irish kings: the beginning of English lordship in Ireland.
    • Saladin Ends the Fatimid CaliphateA.D. 1171, Cairo, Egypt. Saladin, the Kurdish nephew of Shirkuh, had become vizier to the last Fatimid caliph, al-Adid, in 1169 as officer of the Sunni Nur al-Din. In September 1171 he had the Friday prayer said in Cairo for the Abbasid caliph of Baghdad; days later al-Adid died. Two centuries of Shi'ite rule over Egypt ended without a blow, and Saladin became master of the Nile.
    • The Lombard League at LegnanoA.D. 1176, Legnano, Lombardy. On 29 May 1176 the foot soldiers of Milan and the Lombard cities, rallied round their carroccio, the great ox-drawn standard wagon, withstood the charge of Frederick Barbarossa's knights until their own horsemen returned to rout the Germans. The emperor was unhorsed and thought dead. He made peace with Pope Alexander III at Venice, and the cities kept their liberties.
    • The Cham Fleet Sacks AngkorA.D. 1177, Yasodharapura, Cambodia. Jaya Indravarman IV, king of Champa, sent a war fleet up the Mekong and across the Great Lake to Yasodharapura. The Khmer usurper Tribhuvanadityavarman was slain and the city plundered. The shame was avenged by a Khmer prince, Jayavarman, who drove the Cham out in a battle on the lake that the reliefs of the Bayon still show in grim detail.
    • Jayavarman VII CrownedA.D. 1181, Angkor Thom, Cambodia. Crowned after his war against the Cham, Jayavarman VII made Mahayana Buddhism the faith of the court and built as no Khmer king before him. He walled Angkor Thom with a moat and five gates crowned by great faces, set the Bayon with its fifty-odd face towers at its heart, and lined the royal roads with 121 rest houses and 102 hospitals.
    • The Battle of Dan-no-uraA.D. 1185, Dan-no-ura, Shimonoseki, Japan. In the strait of Shimonoseki the Minamoto fleet under Minamoto no Yoshitsune met the last ships of the Taira. When the tide turned against them the Taira were destroyed, and the grandmother of the boy Emperor Antoku took him in her arms and leapt into the sea with the sacred sword. The Genpei War was over, and the warriors ruled Japan.
