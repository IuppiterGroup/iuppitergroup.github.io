    The chronicle of these years

    • Ban Chao in the Western RegionsA.D. 91, Kucha, Xinjiang, China. Ban Chao, brother of the historian Ban Gu, had gone west with thirty-six men and by daring and diplomacy brought the Tarim states back under Han. In 91 he was made Protector General at Kucha; he had already turned back a Kushan army, and in 97 he sent his envoy Gan Ying toward Rome, who halted at the shore of a great western sea.
    • The Buddha Takes Human Formc. A.D. 100, Mathura, Uttar Pradesh, India. Earlier artists had shown the Buddha only by signs: a footprint, an empty throne, a tree. Under the Kushans the sculptors of Gandhara, schooled in Greek and Roman forms, carved him as a serene young man in a robe of heavy folds with waving hair, while the workshops of Mathura shaped him in red sandstone after the Indian manner. Both images travelled with the faith to China.
    • Cai Lun Presents PaperA.D. 105, Luoyang, Henan, China. Cai Lun, a eunuch in charge of the imperial workshops, presented to Emperor He a writing sheet made from tree bark, hemp ends, rags, and old fishing nets, beaten to pulp and dried on a screen. Cruder papers had existed before, but his method made paper cheap and good, and over the next thousand years it carried learning across Asia and into Europe.
    • Trajan Conquers DaciaA.D. 106, Sarmizegetusa Regia, Dacia. In two hard wars Trajan bridged the Danube at Drobeta and broke the Dacian kingdom of Decebalus, who killed himself rather than be taken; his head was brought to Rome. The gold of Dacia paid for Trajan's Forum and the great column whose spiral reliefs tell the story, and Roman settlers gave the land its Latin speech.
    • The Empire at Its HeightA.D. 117, Selinus, Cilicia. Trajan, having added Dacia and pressed his legions to the Persian Gulf, dies on the road home at Selinus in Cilicia. The Empire now reaches from Britain to Mesopotamia, the widest the Roman eagles will ever fly.
    • Hadrian's WallA.D. 122, Housesteads, Britain. Hadrian, who spent half his reign touring the frontiers, came to Britain and ordered a wall built from the Tyne to the Solway, eighty Roman miles, to separate the Romans from the barbarians. Of stone and turf, with milecastles, turrets, and forts like Housesteads, it marked the limit of the empire for nearly three centuries.
    • Gautamiputra Satakarni Destroys the Kshaharatasc. A.D. 124, Nashik, Maharashtra, India. Gautamiputra Satakarni, king of the Satavahanas of the Deccan, overthrew the Saka satrap Nahapana and restored his house's power from the Narmada to the Krishna. In the Jogalthembi hoard thousands of Nahapana's silver coins were found restruck with Gautamiputra's name. His mother Gautami Balashri praised him in a cave inscription at Nashik as destroyer of Sakas, Yavanas, and Pahlavas.
    • Kanishka the Greatc. A.D. 127, Purushapura (Peshawar), Pakistan. Kanishka, greatest of the Kushan emperors, ruled from the Oxus to the Ganges with his winter capital at Purushapura. His Rabatak inscription tells that he issued his edicts in the Bactrian tongue and that his rule reached Pataliputra and Champa. Buddhists remember him as a second Ashoka: he raised a towering stupa at Purushapura, and his coins bear one of the first images of the Buddha.
