    The chronicle of these years

    • Spartacus Breaks Out at Capua73 B.C., Capua, Campania. Seventy-odd gladiators led by Spartacus, a Thracian, broke out of their training school at Capua with kitchen knives and spits, took refuge on Vesuvius, and raised an army of runaway slaves that beat several Roman armies. Marcus Licinius Crassus crushed them in 71, and six thousand captives were crucified along the Appian Way.
    • Pompey Enters the Temple63 B.C., Jerusalem, Judea. Called in to settle the war between the Hasmonean brothers Hyrcanus II and Aristobulus II, Pompey besieged the Temple Mount for three months and took it, entering the Holy of Holies, which he found empty of any image. He left the treasury untouched but made Judea tributary to Rome and set Hyrcanus over it as high priest.
    • The Battle of Carrhae53 B.C., Carrhae, Mesopotamia. Marcus Licinius Crassus, seeking glory to match Caesar and Pompey, marched into Mesopotamia and was met on the open plain by the Parthian general Surena with mounted archers and armored lancers, supplied with arrows by a train of camels. Crassus's son died with his cavalry, Crassus himself was killed at a parley, and some twenty thousand Romans fell and ten thousand were taken.
    • The Siege of Alesia52 B.C., Alesia, Gaul. Vercingetorix, chief of the Arverni, united the Gauls in a last great revolt. Caesar shut him up in the hill town of Alesia behind eleven miles of works and beat off from an outer ring a vast army of relief. Vercingetorix rode out and laid his arms at Caesar's feet, and Gaul was Roman.
    • Caesar Crosses the Rubicon49 B.C., Rubicon River, Italy. Ordered by the Senate to give up his army, Caesar stood on the bank of the Rubicon, the little stream that marked the border of his province and of Italy, and crossed with a single legion, saying, 'The die is cast.' Pompey and most of the Senate fled Rome, and the civil war that would end the Roman Republic had begun.
    • The Battle of Pharsalus48 B.C., Pharsalus, Thessaly. Pompey, with twice Caesar's infantry and seven times his horse, gave battle at Pharsalus. Caesar posted a fourth line of picked cohorts, who stopped Pompey's cavalry by thrusting their javelins at the riders' faces, and the senatorial army broke. Pompey fled to Egypt, where the ministers of the boy king Ptolemy XIII had him stabbed as he stepped ashore.
    • The Ides of March44 B.C., Curia of Pompey, Rome. Caesar, named dictator for life, was stabbed twenty-three times on the Ides of March at a meeting of the Senate in the hall beside the Theater of Pompey by some sixty conspirators led by Marcus Brutus and Gaius Cassius. He fell at the foot of Pompey's statue. The conspirators cried liberty, but the people mourned Caesar, and Antony turned them against the assassins.
    • The Battles of Philippi42 B.C., Philippi, Macedonia. In two battles on the plain of Philippi in October, Antony and Octavian defeated the armies of the Liberators. Cassius, believing all lost, killed himself after the first; Brutus after the second, saying that he had found no man who was not true to him. With them died the last army of the Republic.
