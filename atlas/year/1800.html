    The chronicle of these years

    • Bonaparte at the PyramidsA.D. 1798, Embabeh, Egypt. Napoleon Bonaparte landed in Egypt with some thirty-five thousand men and a commission of scholars. On 21 July 1798 at Embabeh on the Nile, in sight of the pyramids, his squares broke the charges of Murad Bey's Mamluk cavalry, and Cairo was taken. Eleven days later Nelson destroyed the French fleet in Aboukir Bay, and the army was marooned in Egypt.
    • The Coup of BrumaireA.D. 1799, Saint-Cloud, France. Returning secretly from Egypt, Napoleon Bonaparte joined the Director Sieyès in a plot against the discredited Directory. On 18 and 19 Brumaire, 9 and 10 November 1799, the councils were moved to Saint-Cloud, where grenadiers cleared the hall of the Five Hundred. Bonaparte became First Consul, and the Revolution had found its master.
    • The Fall of SeringapatamA.D. 1799, Srirangapatna, Karnataka, India. In the Fourth Mysore War the Company's armies under General Harris besieged Tipu Sultan in his island fortress on the Kaveri. On 4 May 1799 the British stormed the breach in the heat of the afternoon, and Tipu, fighting at a gateway, fell among the dead. The Wodeyar dynasty was restored to a reduced Mysore, and the last great rival of the British in the south was gone.
    • Ranjit Singh Takes LahoreA.D. 1799, Lahore, Punjab, Pakistan. Ranjit Singh, an eighteen-year-old Sikh chief who had lost an eye to smallpox, rode into Lahore in July 1799, and in 1801 was proclaimed Maharaja of the Punjab. With an army later drilled by French and Italian veterans of Napoleon, he united the Sikh confederacies, took Multan, Kashmir, and Peshawar from the Afghans, and made the Koh-i-Noor the jewel of his treasury.
    • The Dutch East India Company DissolvedA.D. 1799, Batavia, Java. Ruined by corruption, war with Britain, and the debts of its Asian trade, the Dutch East India Company, once the richest corporation in the world, saw its charter lapse on 31 December 1799. Its possessions passed to the Batavian Republic, and the Indies became a colony of the Dutch state, soon to be shaken by the wars of Napoleon.
    • Bonaparte at MarengoA.D. 1800, Marengo, Piedmont. On 14 June 1800 the First Consul, who had crossed the Great St. Bernard with his reserve army in May, was all but beaten on the plain of Marengo by the Austrian general Melas, until Desaix came up in the afternoon and the younger Kellermann's cavalry charge broke the Austrian column. Desaix fell in the counterstroke. Melas signed away Piedmont and Lombardy, and Bonaparte's consulship was secured.
    • The Concordat with RomeA.D. 1801, Paris, France. On 15 July 1801 Bonaparte's envoys and Cardinal Consalvi signed the Concordat, which recognized the Catholic faith as the religion of the great majority of Frenchmen and closed the schism of the Revolution. Pius VII accepted the loss of the Church's lands, the First Consul gained the naming of bishops, and the restored worship was celebrated with a Te Deum at Notre-Dame on Easter Sunday 1802.
    • Gia Long Unites VietnamA.D. 1802, Hue, Vietnam. Nguyen Phuc Anh, heir of the Nguyen lords, who had fought the Tay Son for twenty-five years with Siamese, French, and Chinese help, took Phu Xuan in 1801 and Thang Long in 1802. Reigning as Emperor Gia Long from Hue, he ruled from the Chinese border to the Gulf of Siam, and in 1804 the Qing court recognized the country under the name Viet Nam.
