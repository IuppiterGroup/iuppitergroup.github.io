    The chronicle of these years

    • The Serbs Rise at OrašacA.D. 1804, Orašac, Serbia. After the janissary chiefs, the dahije, murdered the leading Serbian knezes of the pashalik of Belgrade in the winter of 1804, the elders gathered at Orašac in February and chose the livestock trader Karađorđe Petrović as their leader. The rising against the janissaries became a war against the Ottoman Porte itself, and by the beginning of 1807 the rebels held Belgrade.
    • The Civil Code of the FrenchA.D. 1804, Paris, France. Promulgated on 21 March 1804 after debates over which the First Consul himself often presided, the Civil Code gathered the customs of the old provinces and the reforms of the Revolution into 2,281 articles on persons, property, and contract. Equality before the law, civil marriage, and the end of feudal tenure traveled with the French armies, and the Code, much amended, still governs France and Belgium and shaped the law of much of the world.
    • Napoleon Crowns Himself EmperorA.D. 1804, Notre-Dame, Paris. In Notre-Dame on 2 December 1804, with Pope Pius VII come from Rome to anoint him, Napoleon took the crown from the altar and set it on his own head, then crowned Joséphine kneeling before him. The Senate and a plebiscite had made the First Consul hereditary Emperor of the French that spring; Beethoven, hearing the news, had torn the dedication to Bonaparte from his Third Symphony.
    • Usman dan Fodio's Holy WarA.D. 1804, Sokoto, Hausaland. Usman dan Fodio, a Fulani scholar who preached against the laxity of the Hausa kings, withdrew from Gobir with his followers in 1804, as the Prophet had withdrawn to Medina, and was proclaimed Commander of the Faithful. His holy war overthrew the Hausa states, and from Sokoto, founded in 1809, his son Muhammad Bello and their heirs ruled the largest state in West Africa, a great slaving power, until the British came in 1903.
    • Haiti Proclaims Its IndependenceA.D. 1804, Gonaïves, Haiti. Napoleon sent his brother-in-law General Leclerc with twenty thousand veterans to restore slavery in Saint-Domingue; Toussaint Louverture was seized and died in a French fortress, but yellow fever and the armies of Jean-Jacques Dessalines destroyed the French, who surrendered after Vertières in November 1803. At Gonaïves on 1 January 1804 Dessalines proclaimed Haiti, the first nation founded by former slaves, and that spring had the remaining French colonists put to death.
    • Nelson at TrafalgarA.D. 1805, Cape Trafalgar, Spain. Off Cape Trafalgar on 21 October 1805, Vice-Admiral Lord Nelson led twenty-seven ships of the line in two columns straight through the Franco-Spanish fleet of Admiral Villeneuve, taking or destroying twenty-two of thirty-three without losing one. A sharpshooter's ball from the Redoutable killed Nelson on the Victory's quarterdeck; Britain's command of the seas went unchallenged for a century.
    • The Sun of AusterlitzA.D. 1805, Austerlitz, Moravia. On 2 December 1805, the first anniversary of his coronation, Napoleon drew the Russian and Austrian armies of Alexander I and Francis II down from the Pratzen Heights against his weakened right, then struck their abandoned center out of the morning mist. The allied army was broken in a day. Austria made peace at Pressburg, ceding Venetia and the Tyrol, and the Third Coalition dissolved.
    • Muhammad Ali Made Pasha of EgyptA.D. 1805, Cairo, Egypt. Muhammad Ali, an Albanian officer from Kavala who had come to Egypt with the Ottoman force sent against the French, won over the ulema and people of Cairo and was named pasha by the sultan in 1805. On 1 March 1811 he invited the Mamluk beys to a feast in the Citadel and had them shot down in a narrow lane as they left. He made Egypt the strongest power in the Muslim world.
