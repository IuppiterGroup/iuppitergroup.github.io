    The chronicle of these years

    • The Hymns of the Rigvedac. 1500 B.C., The Punjab, Pakistan. Herding tribes speaking an Indo-Aryan tongue came over the passes into the land of seven rivers and there composed the Rigveda: 1,028 hymns to Indra, Agni, Varuna, and Soma, in a Sanskrit kin to Greek and Latin. Learned by heart and handed down by priestly families with flawless exactness for three thousand years, the hymns are the oldest scripture still in use.
    • The Mycenaeans Take Knossosc. 1450 B.C., Knossos, Crete. The palaces of Crete burned, and Greek-speaking lords from the mainland took Knossos. Their scribes adapted the Minoan script into Linear B to write early Greek, a script Michael Ventris deciphered in 1952. From this time the Aegean belonged to the Mycenaeans.
    • The Exodus and the Law at Sinaic. 1446 B.C., Mount Sinai. Four hundred and eighty years before Solomon began the Temple, by the reckoning of the First Book of Kings, Moses led the children of Israel out of Egypt after the plagues and the first Passover. They crossed the sea on dry ground, and at Mount Sinai God gave them the Law and the Ten Commandments.
    • The Alphabet of Ugaritc. 1400 B.C., Ugarit, Syria. The scribes of Ugarit pressed an alphabet of thirty wedge-shaped letters into clay and wrote with it the myths of Baal and El, the epics of Kirta and Aqhat, letters, and accounts. Tablets for schoolboys set the letters in nearly the order of the later Hebrew alphabet.
    • Ashur-uballit Frees Assyria1363 B.C., Ashur, Assyria. Ashur-uballit I threw off the overlordship of Mitanni, wrote to Pharaoh as a great king and a brother, and married his daughter to the Kassite king of Babylon. From his reign Assyria was a great power, and the city state of merchants became the kingdom that would one day rule the Near East.
    • The Lapita Potters of the Bismarck Seac. 1350 B.C., Mussau Island, Papua New Guinea. In the Bismarck Archipelago appeared a people who stamped their pottery with fine toothed patterns of faces and geometric bands, raised houses on stilts over the lagoons, and sailed outrigger canoes. These were the Lapita folk, Austronesians from Island Southeast Asia, and within a few centuries their voyages would carry them into the empty ocean beyond the Solomons.
    • Letters of the Great Kingsc. 1350 B.C., Amarna, Egypt. The archive of Amarna, found in 1887, holds the letters that the kings of Babylon, Assyria, Mitanni, Hatti, and Alashiya wrote to Amenhotep III and Akhenaten in Akkadian, as brothers and equals, along with the pleas of Canaanite vassals such as Rib-Hadda of Byblos against the Habiru and Amurru.
    • Akhenaten Founds Amarnac. 1346 B.C., Amarna, Egypt. Amenhotep IV turned from Amun and the old gods to the sole worship of the sun disk, the Aten, took the name Akhenaten, and built a new capital, Akhetaten, on virgin ground halfway between Thebes and Memphis. In its ruins were found the Amarna letters, cuneiform tablets of the pharaoh's correspondence with Babylon, Mitanni, Assyria, and the princes of Canaan.
