    The chronicle of these years

    • The Battle on the IceA.D. 1242, Lake Peipus, Rus'. On 5 April 1242 Prince Alexander Nevsky of Novgorod met the knights of the Livonian Order and the bishop of Dorpat's men on the frozen lake between Pskov and Estonia. Their wedge of horsemen broke against the Novgorod foot and was driven back across the ice. The Order's drive into Russian lands was ended, and the border of Latin and Orthodox Christendom was fixed for centuries.
    • The Mongols at Köse DağA.D. 1243, Köse Dağ, Anatolia. The Mongol general Baiju, pressing west from Persia, met the army of the Seljuk sultan of Rum, Kaykhusraw II, at Köse Dağ east of Sivas on 26 June 1243. The Seljuk host, swollen with Georgian, Greek, and Latin mercenaries, broke at the first onset. Rum became a Mongol tributary, and in its decay the Turkish frontier beyliks, the Ottomans among them, would rise.
    • Sakya Pandita Meets KötenA.D. 1247, Liangzhou, Gansu, China. Summoned by Köten, grandson of Genghis Khan, the abbot Sakya Pandita made the long journey to Liangzhou, where he agreed to the submission of Tibet, cured the prince of an illness, and was made his agent over Central Tibet. His nephew Phagpa became the teacher of Kublai, and the bond of lama and patron between Tibet and the Mongol khans began.
    • Ferdinand III Takes SevilleA.D. 1248, Seville, Andalusia. After a siege of fifteen months, in which the Castilian fleet under Ramón Bonifaz broke the bridge of boats that fed the city, Seville surrendered to Ferdinand III on 23 November 1248. Its Muslim people departed for Granada and Africa. Of al-Andalus there remained only the emirate of Granada, a vassal of Castile, which would hold out for nearly two and a half centuries.
    • The Mamluks Seize EgyptA.D. 1250, Mansura, Egypt. King Louis IX of France took Damietta in 1249, but in February 1250 his brother Robert of Artois charged into the streets of Mansura and was slain by the Bahri Mamluks, the slave guards of the late sultan al-Salih Ayyub. Louis was captured in the retreat and ransomed. In May the Mamluks murdered the new sultan Turanshah, and Egypt passed into the hands of its slave soldiers.
    • The Sun Temple of Konarkc. A.D. 1250, Konark, Odisha, India. Narasimhadeva I of the Eastern Ganga dynasty, who had carried the war against the Muslims of Bengal into their own land, built at Konark on the Orissa coast a temple to the Sun in the form of the god's chariot, drawn by seven stone horses on twenty-four carved wheels. Its great tower, a landmark for sailors who called it the Black Pagoda, fell in later centuries.
    • The Mongols Sack BaghdadA.D. 1258, Baghdad, Iraq. Hulagu, brother of the Great Khan Möngke, having reduced the Assassin castles, summoned the caliph al-Musta'sim to submit. When he temporized, the Mongols stormed Baghdad in February 1258 and sacked it for a week; the dead were reckoned in the hundreds of thousands. The last Abbasid caliph of Baghdad was rolled in a carpet and trampled by horses, and the Tigris, it was said, ran black with the ink of its libraries.
    • The Battle of Ain JalutA.D. 1260, Ain Jalut, Galilee. When the death of the Great Khan drew Hulagu east with most of his army, the Mamluk sultan Qutuz marched out of Egypt against the force left in Syria under Kitbuqa. On 3 September 1260, at the Spring of Goliath in the valley of Jezreel, the Mamluks drew the Mongols into an ambush and destroyed them; Kitbuqa was slain. The westward tide of the Mongol conquest was turned for good.
