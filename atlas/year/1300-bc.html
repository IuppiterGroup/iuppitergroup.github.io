    The chronicle of these years

    • The Lapita Potters of the Bismarck Seac. 1350 B.C., Mussau Island, Papua New Guinea. In the Bismarck Archipelago appeared a people who stamped their pottery with fine toothed patterns of faces and geometric bands, raised houses on stilts over the lagoons, and sailed outrigger canoes. These were the Lapita folk, Austronesians from Island Southeast Asia, and within a few centuries their voyages would carry them into the empty ocean beyond the Solomons.
    • Akhenaten Founds Amarnac. 1346 B.C., Amarna, Egypt. Amenhotep IV turned from Amun and the old gods to the sole worship of the sun disk, the Aten, took the name Akhenaten, and built a new capital, Akhetaten, on virgin ground halfway between Thebes and Memphis. In its ruins were found the Amarna letters, cuneiform tablets of the pharaoh's correspondence with Babylon, Mitanni, Assyria, and the princes of Canaan.
    • Suppiluliuma Breaks Mitannic. 1340 B.C., Washukanni, Syria. The Hittite king Suppiluliuma I crossed the Euphrates, sacked the Mitannian capital Washukanni, and stripped King Tushratta of his Syrian vassals. When Tushratta was murdered by one of his sons, Suppiluliuma set the fugitive prince Shattiwaza on a vassal throne, and the Hittites stood supreme from central Anatolia to the Euphrates.
    • Tutankhamun Restores the Old Godsc. 1332 B.C., Valley of the Kings, Egypt. The boy king Tutankhamun, raised under Akhenaten, abandoned Amarna, reopened the temples of Amun and the old gods, and proclaimed the restoration on a stele at Karnak. He died young, and his small tomb in the Valley of the Kings, found nearly intact by Howard Carter in 1922, made him the most famous pharaoh.
    • The Ship of Uluburunc. 1305 B.C., Uluburun, Lycia. A merchant ship sank off the Lycian coast carrying ten tons of Cypriot copper and a ton of tin, the makings of eleven tons of bronze, with glass ingots, ebony, ivory, amber, Canaanite jars, and a gold scarab of Nefertiti. Found by a sponge diver in 1982, it is the richest cargo of the Bronze Age.
    • Chariots at Kadesh1274 B.C., Kadesh, Syria. Ramesses II and the Hittite king Muwatalli II meet at Kadesh in the greatest chariot battle of antiquity, perhaps five thousand chariots in all. Caught in an ambush, Ramesses cuts his way out and carves a great victory on the temple walls of Egypt; in truth the field was a draw. Fifteen years later the two powers sign one of the oldest peace treaties on record.
    • Abu Simbel Cut from the Cliffc. 1264 B.C., Abu Simbel, Nubia. In Nubia, Ramesses II had a temple cut deep into a sandstone cliff, fronted by four seated colossi of himself twenty meters high. Twice a year the rising sun lights the gods in its inmost sanctuary. In the 1960s the whole temple was cut apart and raised above the waters of Lake Nasser.
    • Ramesses II Makes Peace with the Hittites1259 B.C., Pi-Ramesses, Egypt. Fifteen years after Kadesh, Ramesses II and the Hittite king Hattusili III swore an alliance of mutual defense, extradition of fugitives, and brotherhood between their houses, recorded in hieroglyphs at Karnak and in Akkadian on clay tablets at Hattusa. It is the oldest peace treaty whose text survives from both sides, and Ramesses later married Hattusili's daughter.
