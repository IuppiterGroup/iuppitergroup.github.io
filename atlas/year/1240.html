    The chronicle of these years

    • Sundiata Triumphs at Kirinac. A.D. 1235, Kirina, Mali. Sundiata Keita, the lame prince of the Malinke whose story the griots still sing, gathered the clans of the upper Niger against Sumanguru Kante, the smith-king of Sosso, who had overrun the remnants of Ghana. At Kirina Sumanguru was broken, and Sundiata, hailed as mansa, founded the empire of Mali, whose laws, the griots say, were proclaimed at Kurukan Fuga.
    • Razia, Sultan of DelhiA.D. 1236, Delhi, India. Iltutmish named his daughter Razia his heir over his sons, and in 1236 the people of Delhi raised her to the throne. She put off the veil, rode an elephant before her troops, and held court in a man's coat and cap. The Turkish nobles would not bear her favour to the Abyssinian Yaqut; they rose against her, and in 1240 she was killed in flight.
    • The Dawn of Sukhothaic. A.D. 1238, Sukhothai, Thailand. Two Tai chiefs, Pho Khun Bang Klang Hao and Pho Khun Pha Mueang, expelled the Khmer governor of Sukhothai, and Bang Klang Hao reigned there as Si Inthrathit. Thai tradition honors his kingdom as the first Thai state, where Theravada Buddhism, free trade, and a fatherly kingship joined to form the model of later Siam.
    • The Mongols Sack KievA.D. 1240, Kiev, Rus'. Batu's Mongols, having burned Ryazan and Vladimir, stormed Kiev, the mother of Russian cities, on 6 December 1240 after battering down its walls. The last defenders died around the Church of the Tithes, which collapsed beneath the crowd sheltering on its vaults. Six years later the papal envoy John of Plano Carpini found scarcely two hundred houses standing.
    • Batu Crushes the Hungarians at MohiA.D. 1241, Mohi, Hungary. Two days after another Mongol army destroyed the Silesian and Polish knights at Liegnitz, Batu and Subutai forced the bridge over the Sajó and surrounded the camp of Béla IV on the plain of Mohi on 11 April 1241, leaving a gap through which the fleeing Hungarians were cut down by the thousand. Hungary was ravaged for a year, until news of the Great Khan's death drew the Mongols back east.
    • The Battle on the IceA.D. 1242, Lake Peipus, Rus'. On 5 April 1242 Prince Alexander Nevsky of Novgorod met the knights of the Livonian Order and the bishop of Dorpat's men on the frozen lake between Pskov and Estonia. Their wedge of horsemen broke against the Novgorod foot and was driven back across the ice. The Order's drive into Russian lands was ended, and the border of Latin and Orthodox Christendom was fixed for centuries.
    • The Mongols at Köse DağA.D. 1243, Köse Dağ, Anatolia. The Mongol general Baiju, pressing west from Persia, met the army of the Seljuk sultan of Rum, Kaykhusraw II, at Köse Dağ east of Sivas on 26 June 1243. The Seljuk host, swollen with Georgian, Greek, and Latin mercenaries, broke at the first onset. Rum became a Mongol tributary, and in its decay the Turkish frontier beyliks, the Ottomans among them, would rise.
    • Sakya Pandita Meets KötenA.D. 1247, Liangzhou, Gansu, China. Summoned by Köten, grandson of Genghis Khan, the abbot Sakya Pandita made the long journey to Liangzhou, where he agreed to the submission of Tibet, cured the prince of an illness, and was made his agent over Central Tibet. His nephew Phagpa became the teacher of Kublai, and the bond of lama and patron between Tibet and the Mongol khans began.
