    The chronicle of these years

    • Roman Ships at Muzirisc. A.D. 50, Pattanam, Kerala, India. Greek pilots had learned to ride the southwest monsoon straight across the Arabian Sea, and each summer ships from Roman Egypt reached Muziris on the Malabar coast for pepper, pearls, and fine cloth. Pliny grumbled that India drained the empire of fifty million sesterces a year, and hoards of Roman gold and silver coins are still turned up in southern India.
    • The Apostle Thomas in IndiaA.D. 52, Kodungallur, Kerala, India. The Apostle Thomas, as the Christians of Malabar have always held, landed at Muziris in A.D. 52, preached the Gospel along the coast, and founded seven churches. He was martyred at Mylapore near Madras in A.D. 72, and his tomb there drew pilgrims for centuries; Marco Polo saw it. The Saint Thomas Christians of Kerala honour him as their father in the faith.
    • The Revolt of Boudicac. A.D. 60, Londinium, Britain. When the Romans seized the kingdom of the Iceni, flogged its queen Boudica, and outraged her daughters, she raised the tribes of eastern Britain and burned Camulodunum, Londinium, and Verulamium, killing tens of thousands. The governor Suetonius Paulinus, hurrying back from the slaughter of the Druids on Mona, crushed her vast host with some ten thousand men, and Boudica took poison.
    • The Great Fire and Nero's PersecutionA.D. 64, Circus Maximus, Rome. Fire broke out by the Circus Maximus and burned for six days, leaving only four of Rome's fourteen districts untouched. Rumor blamed Nero, who built his Golden House on the ruins, and he turned the blame on the Christians, who were torn by dogs and burned as torches in his gardens. In these years, by ancient tradition, Peter and Paul died as martyrs in Rome.
    • The White Horse TempleA.D. 68, Luoyang, Henan, China. Emperor Ming, the story goes, dreamed of a golden man flying before his palace and sent envoys west to seek him. They returned with the Indian monks Kasyapa Matanga and Dharmaratna and sutras carried on a white horse, and the emperor built for them outside Luoyang the White Horse Temple, by tradition the first Buddhist temple in China.
    • Titus Destroys the TempleA.D. 70, Jerusalem, Judea. Four years into the Jewish revolt, Titus, son of Vespasian, besieged Jerusalem with four legions, starved it, and stormed it after five months. The Temple was burned in August, the city destroyed, and its treasures, the seven-branched lampstand among them, carried in the triumph shown on the Arch of Titus. Judaism endured, without its Temple, in the schools of the rabbis.
    • Vesuvius Buries PompeiiA.D. 79, Pompeii, Campania. Vesuvius, long thought extinct, erupted and buried Pompeii, Herculaneum, and Stabiae under ash and pumice. Pliny the Elder, admiral of the fleet at Misenum, sailed to the rescue and died on the shore; his nephew, Pliny the Younger, described the cloud shaped like a pine. The buried towns, uncovered since the eighteenth century, show the daily life of a Roman city as no book can.
    • The Colosseum OpensA.D. 80, Colosseum, Rome. Titus dedicated the Flavian Amphitheater, built by his father Vespasian on the drained lake of Nero's Golden House, with a hundred days of games in which some nine thousand animals were killed. Holding perhaps fifty thousand spectators beneath its awnings, it was the greatest arena of the Roman world.
