    The chronicle of these years

    • Pakal the Great of PalenqueA.D. 615, Palenque, Mexico. K'inich Janaab Pakal took the throne of Palenque at the age of twelve and reigned for sixty-eight years, raising a city of graceful palaces and temples on a shelf above the lowland plain. In 1952 the archaeologist Alberto Ruz found his tomb deep beneath the Temple of the Inscriptions, the king laid in a jade mask under a sarcophagus lid carved with his descent into the underworld.
    • Ishanavarman's City of Brickc. A.D. 616, Sambor Prei Kuk, Cambodia. Ishanavarman I, king of Chenla, ruled from Ishanapura on the Stung Sen, where more than a hundred brick towers rose within moated enclosures, some carved with the flying palaces of the gods. The Chinese knew him as lord of thirty walled towns of several thousand households each; his city, now Sambor Prei Kuk, was the greatest of Cambodia before Angkor.
    • Tang Rises from the Ruin of SuiA.D. 618, Chang'an, Shaanxi, China. With rebellion everywhere, Emperor Yang was strangled by his own guards at Jiangdu, and Li Yuan, Duke of Tang, who had marched from Taiyuan and taken Chang'an, proclaimed himself emperor. His son Li Shimin then beat the rival warlords one by one. The Tang, with its open and cosmopolitan court, became the golden age long remembered in East Asia.
    • Pulakeshin II Halts Harshac. A.D. 618, Narmada River, India. Harsha, master of the north, marched on the Deccan, but the Chalukya king Pulakeshin II met him on the Narmada and threw him back. The Aihole inscription of 634 boasts that Harsha's joy melted away with fear when he saw his elephants fall in battle. The Narmada became the frontier between the two empires, and Pulakeshin took the title Lord of the South.
    • The Hijra to MedinaA.D. 622, Medina, Arabia. Warned of a plot against his life, Muhammad slipped out of Mecca with Abu Bakr and reached the oasis of Yathrib, thereafter Medina, the City of the Prophet, in September 622. There he became lawgiver, judge, and war leader of a community bound by faith rather than blood. The Muslim calendar counts its years from this emigration.
    • Muhammad's Victory at BadrA.D. 624, Badr, Arabia. At the wells of Badr, some 313 Muslims fell upon a Meccan force about three times their number that had come out to guard a rich caravan led by Abu Sufyan. Abu Jahl and other chiefs of the Quraysh were slain. The victory, won in the month of Ramadan, was taken by the believers as a sign of divine favor and made Medina a power in Arabia.
    • The Avars and Persians Before ConstantinopleA.D. 626, Constantinople, Thrace. In the summer of 626, while Heraclius campaigned far off in the Caucasus, the Avar khagan and his Slav subjects invested the Theodosian Walls, and the Persian general Shahrbaraz watched from Chalcedon across the Bosporus. Patriarch Sergius carried the icon of the Virgin along the ramparts; the imperial fleet sank the Slav canoes in the Golden Horn, and the khagan burned his engines and withdrew.
    • Brahmagupta Defines ZeroA.D. 628, Bhinmal, Rajasthan, India. At Bhillamala in Rajasthan the astronomer Brahmagupta completed the Brahmasphutasiddhanta, the first book to treat zero as a number in its own right, with rules for adding, subtracting, and multiplying by it, and for reckoning with negative numbers as debts. Carried to Baghdad and rendered into Arabic, the Indian numerals with their place value and zero passed to Europe as the Arabic figures.
