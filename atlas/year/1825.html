    The chronicle of these years

    • The Massacre of ChiosA.D. 1822, Chios, Aegean. When the Greeks of the Morea rose in 1821 and slaughtered the Muslims and Jews of Tripolitsa, the sultan's vengeance fell elsewhere. In April 1822, after a band of Samians raised revolt on the rich island of Chios, an Ottoman fleet landed troops who killed perhaps twenty thousand Christians and sold some forty-five thousand into slavery. Delacroix's painting of the massacre roused all Europe for the Greek cause.
    • The First Anglo-Burmese WarA.D. 1824, Rangoon, Burma. Burma's conquests of Arakan, Manipur, and Assam brought it to the borders of British Bengal. In May 1824 a British fleet seized Rangoon, and after two years of war, in which the great general Maha Bandula was killed by a shell at Danubyu, King Bagyidaw signed the Treaty of Yandabo in 1826, giving up Assam, Manipur, Arakan, and Tenasserim and paying a million pounds.
    • The Straits DividedA.D. 1824, Malacca, Malaya. By the Anglo-Dutch Treaty of London in March 1824 the Netherlands gave up Malacca and all claims on the Malay Peninsula and Singapore, and Britain gave up Bencoolen and its claims in Sumatra. A line drawn through the Straits of Malacca split the Malay world in two, a division that survives in the frontier between Malaysia and Indonesia.
    • AyacuchoA.D. 1824, Quinua, Peru. On the high plain of Ayacucho on 9 December 1824, the patriot army of Antonio José de Sucre, some six thousand Colombians and Peruvians, met the last royal army of the viceroy José de la Serna. In a few hours the viceroy was wounded and taken and his army surrendered; Spanish rule in South America was at an end, save for a few holdouts that fell by early 1826.
    • Locomotion on the Stockton and DarlingtonA.D. 1825, Darlington, England. On 27 September 1825 George Stephenson drove his engine Locomotion along the new Stockton and Darlington Railway, drawing coal wagons and a coach full of shareholders, on the first public railway to use steam locomotives. Five years later his Rocket ran on the Liverpool and Manchester, and within a generation iron rails had remade the commerce and the warfare of Europe.
    • Diponegoro Raises the Java WarA.D. 1825, Tegalrejo, Java. Prince Diponegoro of Yogyakarta, a mystic who saw himself as the destined just king of the Javanese, rose in July 1825 when the Dutch drove a road across his family's lands at Tegalrejo. His holy war set central Java aflame for five years and cost some two hundred thousand Javanese lives. Lured to a parley at Magelang in 1830, he was seized and exiled.
    • The Destruction of the JanissariesA.D. 1826, Constantinople, Thrace. When the Janissaries, long since a hereditary rabble that made and unmade sultans, overturned their soup kettles in revolt against a new drilled army in June 1826, Mahmud II was ready. The sacred banner of the Prophet was unfurled, his loyal gunners shelled their barracks, and thousands were killed in the flames and the executions that followed. The Ottomans called it the Auspicious Event.
    • Chao Anouvong's WarA.D. 1827, Vientiane, Laos. Chao Anouvong, king of Vientiane and vassal of Siam, marched on Bangkok in 1827, believing a false rumor that Britain was at war with Siam. Turned back at Khorat, where legend credits Thao Suranari with rallying the captives against him, he was pursued to Vientiane. The Siamese razed his city and carried its people across the Mekong, and Anouvong died a prisoner in Bangkok.
