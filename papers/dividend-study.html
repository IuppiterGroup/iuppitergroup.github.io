The Ex-Dividend Day Price Drop Anomaly

Evidence From 19,319 S&P 500 Ex-Dividend Events (2010–2025)

Author John, CaesarBot & luppiter Group
Published April 2026
Revised November 2026
Category Finance · Market Microstructure
References 19 References

This study asks whether stock prices fall by the full dividend on the ex-dividend date, as frictionless pricing predicts. It reruns the analysis on real daily prices from Yahoo Finance for every current S&P 500 company that paid a dividend between 2010 and 2025, counting each company only from the date it joined the index: 19,319 ex-dividend events from 399 firms in 11 sectors. Before any adjustment, shares open lower by 0.777 times the dividend on average. Because the market itself rose slightly on the typical ex-date, the raw figure understates the drop, so the study adds back each stock's expected overnight move, using its estimated beta and the S&P 500 ETF's opening return. The market-adjusted drop averages 0.866 times the dividend (95% CI 0.831 to 0.901, with standard errors clustered by date and firm), a shortfall of about 13% that is statistically significant (t = −7.48) and holds up under the robustness checks in Section 4.2. The October 2026 revision of this paper had estimated a corrected average of about 1.0 from the original paper's own figures; the real data show that estimate was wrong, because it used the market's full-day return rather than its opening move. The shortfall is largest for low-yield stocks (0.798) and smallest for high-yield stocks (0.930), does not differ significantly across sectors or years, and is unrelated to share price. At the stock level, 32 of 343 stocks show a significant shortfall after controlling the false discovery rate, led by AT&T, Cardinal Health, Boeing, and Ventas, while none shows a significantly larger-than-full drop. A dividend capture trade (buy at the close, sell at the ex-day open) earned 9.03 basis points per event before trading costs and taxes.

KEYWORDS: ex-dividend day, price drop ratio, tax clientele effect, dividend capture, market microstructure, clustered inference

SECTION I

1. Introduction

In a frictionless capital market with no taxes and no transaction costs, a stock's price should decline by exactly the amount of the dividend on the ex-dividend date. This prediction follows directly from the absence of arbitrage: if the stock dropped by less than the dividend, an investor could buy shares the day before, capture the dividend, and sell at a profit; if it dropped by more, an investor could short before and cover after. The Miller & Modigliani (1961) dividend irrelevance proposition provides the theoretical foundation: in perfect markets, dividend policy is irrelevant to firm value, and the ex-day price adjustment should be dollar-for-dollar.

Yet real markets are not frictionless. At least three classes of friction may cause the observed price drop to deviate from the dividend amount:

  1. Differential taxation. In most jurisdictions, dividends and capital gains are taxed at different rates. If the marginal investor faces a higher tax rate on dividends than on capital gains, the equilibrium ex-day price drop will be less than the full dividend, reflecting the after-tax indifference condition. Elton & Gruber (1970) formalized this insight and used the observed drop ratio to infer the marginal tax rate of stockholders.
  2. Transaction costs and market microstructure. Bid-ask spreads, tick sizes, and order-flow dynamics can distort the observed price change. Frank & Jagannathan (1998) showed that even in Hong Kong, where dividends were tax-free, stock prices dropped by less than the dividend, implicating microstructure effects rather than taxes alone. Graham, Michaely, & Roberts (2003), however, found that as U.S. tick sizes shrank from eighths to sixteenths and then to decimals in 2001, ex-day abnormal returns rose and the median drop ratio fell, which they judged inconsistent with microstructure explanations.
  3. Heterogeneous investors and dividend capture. Institutional investors, short-term traders, and tax-exempt or tax-deferred investors (pension funds and retirement accounts) may have different valuations of dividends versus capital gains. Trading around ex-dates ("dividend capture" strategies) introduces additional price dynamics (Lakonishok & Vermaelen, 1986; Michaely & Vila, 1995).

This paper contributes an updated empirical analysis using 16 years of modern market data (2010–2025), spanning the post-decimalization era and encompassing diverse market conditions including the COVID-19 crash, the 2022 bear market, and the AI-driven bull market. It uses a point-in-time S&P 500 sample, a market adjustment timed to the opening price, standard errors clustered by date and firm, stock-level tests with false discovery rate control, and a direct test of dividend capture.

SECTION II

2. Literature Review

The study of ex-dividend day price behavior dates to Campbell & Beranek (1955), who first documented that stocks tend to drop by less than the dividend amount. The seminal contribution of Elton & Gruber (1970) formalized the tax-clientele explanation: if the marginal investor's tax rate on dividends exceeds that on capital gains, the equilibrium price drop satisfies:

ΔP / D = (1 − τd) / (1 − τg)

where τd and τg are the tax rates on dividend income and capital gains, respectively. They used observed drop ratios to back out implied marginal tax rates and found evidence consistent with the clientele effect: high-yield stocks (held by lower-tax-bracket investors) exhibited drop ratios closer to 1.0.

Black & Scholes (1974) approached the question from the return side. They concluded that, using the best available empirical methods, it is not possible to show that expected returns on high-yield stocks differ from those on low-yield stocks, either before or after taxes, and therefore that the effect of a change in dividend policy on a firm's stock price cannot be determined from the data.

Kalay (1982) challenged the pure tax interpretation, arguing that transaction costs create a band of no-arbitrage around the theoretical drop, making it impossible to precisely identify the marginal investor's tax rate from price data alone.

Lakonishok & Vermaelen (1986) documented abnormal trading volume around ex-dividend dates, consistent with tax-motivated trading and dividend capture strategies. They found that short-term traders systematically trade around ex-dates to exploit differential tax treatment.

Boyd & Jagannathan (1994) proposed a dynamic model where the drop ratio depends on the relative proportions of long-term investors and short-term dividend capturers in the market.

Bali & Hite (1998) argued that discrete tick sizes (1/8ths in the pre-decimalization era) mechanically prevented prices from adjusting by the exact dividend amount, since dividends rarely fell on tick boundaries. They predicted, and found, that the bias was related to the dividend's position within the tick.

Frank & Jagannathan (1998) provided a critical natural experiment: Hong Kong, where dividends were not taxed. Even there, stock prices dropped by only about 50% of the dividend amount. This evidence strongly supports non-tax explanations, particularly market microstructure effects. They proposed a bid-ask explanation: because market makers have a cost advantage in collecting dividends, they tend to buy cum-dividend at the bid and sell after the ex-date at the ask, so cum-day trades cluster at the bid and ex-day trades at the ask, which mechanically reduces the observed drop.

Graham, Michaely, & Roberts (2003) exploited the NYSE's move from eighths to sixteenths and then to decimal pricing, completed in January 2001. The median drop ratio fell from 0.893 in the eighths era to 0.833 in the sixteenths era and 0.750 in the decimal era, and ex-day abnormal returns rose, the opposite of what tick-size explanations predict. They concluded that their results are inconsistent with microstructure explanations and consistent with the tax explanation, which attributes the shortfall to the preferential taxation of capital gains relative to dividend income.

Elton, Gruber, & Blake (2005) compared taxable and non-taxable closed-end funds, finding that the tax status of investors significantly affects the ex-day price drop, providing clean support for the tax-clientele hypothesis.

Cloyd, Li, & Weaver (2006) examined the interaction of tick sizes and tax effects, finding that both independently contribute to the anomaly.

Zhang, Farrell, & Brown (2008) studied the 2003 dividend tax cut (which reduced the top rate on qualified dividends to 15%) and found that the drop ratio increased after the cut, consistent with tax-based explanations.

Rantapuska (2008), using trading data on all investors in the Finnish stock market, found that investors who prefer dividend income buy cum-dividend and sell ex-dividend while investors with the opposite preference do the reverse, and that transaction costs and dividend yield jointly determine whether any short-term ex-day trading occurs.

SECTION III

3. Data and Methodology

3.1 Sample Construction

The sample starts from the companies in the S&P 500 as of November 5, 2026, with the date each was added to the index, taken from the public constituent list maintained in the datasets/s-and-p-500-companies repository on GitHub (sourced from Wikipedia). Companies with two share classes in the index (Alphabet, Fox, and News Corp) contribute one class each, leaving 500 companies, of which 408 paid at least one dividend with an ex-date between January 1, 2010 and December 31, 2025. To keep the sample point-in-time on the way in, an event counts only if its ex-date falls on or after the company's addition to the index; this removes 3,256 events from years before a company joined. Special dividends, defined as a payment more than 2.5 times the median of the company's previous four dividends, are excluded (177 events), since they are typically announced and priced differently from regular dividends. The final sample has 19,319 ex-dividend events from 399 firms, rising from 845 events in 2010 to 1,532 in 2025 as more of today's members join the index and more companies pay dividends. The full ticker list is given in the Data Appendix.

The original version of this paper used 80 hand-picked stocks, 8 per sector, that it never listed, so that sample cannot be rebuilt; the point-in-time S&P 500 sample replaces it. One form of survivorship remains: companies that left the index before November 2026 through acquisition, bankruptcy, or removal are absent, because their price histories are often no longer served and a reused ticker cannot be matched safely to its former company. Section 5.4 discusses what this may do to the estimates.

All prices come from the Yahoo Finance chart service (daily bars, retrieved November 10, 2026), the same source the original paper used through the yfinance library. For each stock we use the daily open and close, which Yahoo adjusts for later stock splits but not for dividends, so the ex-day drop is preserved; the dividend-adjusted close, used only to estimate betas; and the full dividend and split history. Yahoo reports dividend amounts on the same split-adjusted basis as its prices, which we confirmed on known events; for example, Coca-Cola's $0.51 dividend that went ex on December 1, 2025 met a prior close of $73.12 and an ex-day open of $72.60, a drop of $0.52. The SPDR S&P 500 ETF (SPY) serves as the market proxy.

3.2 Variable Construction

For each ex-dividend event, with t the ex-date and t−1 the prior trading day:

DRopen = (Closet−1 − Opent) / D      DRclose = (Closet−1 − Closet) / D

The raw close-to-open ratio mixes the dividend with whatever the market did overnight. Our main measure adds back the stock's expected overnight move:

DRadj = DRopen + β × Rm,open × Closet−1 / D

where Rm,open is SPY's return from the prior close to the ex-day open (adding back SPY's own dividend when the two ex-dates coincide), and β is the stock's beta from an ordinary least squares regression of its daily total returns on SPY's over the 252 trading days ending the day before the ex-date (at least 126 days required; the 32 events without enough history use β = 1). A rising market lifts the ex-day open and shrinks the measured drop, so the market term is added. The original paper subtracted it and used SPY's full-day return; Section 4.3 reports both of those versions for comparison.

Dividend yield is the trailing twelve-month yield: the sum of regular dividends with ex-dates in the 365 days through the event, divided by the prior close. This replaces the original paper's quarterly annualization, which understated the yield of monthly payers. Nominal share price, used in the price-level test, is the split-adjusted close multiplied back by every split that occurred after the event, which recovers the price that actually traded at the time.

3.3 Filtering

As in the original paper, ratios outside [−20, +20] are treated as data errors or news-driven outliers. Because the trim is applied to the outcome itself, we report how many events it removes for each measure (101 for the main measure, 190 for the raw close-to-open ratio) and show the untrimmed results in Section 4.2. Yahoo records the ex-day open as exactly equal to the prior close for 218 events, a share higher than chance would suggest; Section 4.2 also shows results without them.

3.4 Statistical Inference

Events on the same date share the same market shock, and events from the same firm share firm characteristics, so treating them as independent overstates precision (Petersen, 2009). All standard errors, confidence intervals, and t-statistics for means and regressions are therefore clustered two ways, by ex-date and by firm (Cameron, Gelbach, & Miller, 2011). We also report a firm-cluster bootstrap interval (2,000 resamples of whole firms), the Wilcoxon signed-rank test against 1.0, and, for comparison with the original paper, the conventional t-statistic that treats events as independent. Differences across yield terciles, sectors, and years are tested with Wald tests on the same clustered covariance. Stock-level tests use each stock's own events, and the resulting p-values are adjusted for the number of stocks tested with the Benjamini and Hochberg (1995) false discovery rate procedure. To separate real stock-to-stock differences from noise, we also estimate the spread of true stock-level ratios with an empirical Bayes model (Efron & Morris, 1975).

4.3 Why the Market Adjustment's Timing Matters

Market returns on ex-dates were slightly positive on average: SPY's opening return averaged 0.024% and was positive on 55% of ex-dates, while its full-day return averaged 0.062%. Because the market term is multiplied by the price-to-dividend ratio (about 180 for a typical event), even these small returns move the ratio noticeably. Figure 2 shows how the answer depends on the adjustment.

Three points stand out. First, the original paper's adjustment, which subtracted the full-day market term, gives 0.614 on this sample, close to the 0.627 the original reported; this suggests the original computation and data were broadly reproducible, and that its error was the sign. Second, adding the full-day market return back with the correct sign, as the October 2026 revision did arithmetically, gives 0.915 with a median of 1.013, and its interval reaches 1.0 (p = 0.053). That version over-adjusts: it credits the stock with market gains made during the ex-day session, after the opening price was already set. Third, using only the market's overnight move, the return that can actually affect the open, gives 0.862 with β = 1 and 0.866 with estimated betas. The median beta in the sample is 0.93, so estimating betas changes little; the timing of the market return is what matters. The October revision's estimate of about 1.0 therefore came from the timing mismatch, and the real data reverse it.

That margin is the dividend shortfall seen from the other side and is precisely estimated, but it is small: it must cover the bid-ask spread paid on entry and exit, any commissions, and, for a taxable U.S. investor, ordinary income tax on a dividend held too briefly to qualify for the reduced rate (qualified treatment requires holding the shares more than 60 days during the 121-day period that begins 60 days before the ex-date). None of these costs is modeled here, so the result does not show that the trade is profitable after costs.

SECTION V

5. Discussion

5.1 Taxes

Under the Elton and Gruber (1970) framework, a ratio of 0.866 would mean the marginal investor values a dollar of dividends at about 87 cents relative to a dollar of capital gains:

(1 − τd) / (1 − τg) ≈ 0.87

For most U.S. individuals, current law makes that hard to produce. Qualified dividends and long-term capital gains are taxed at the same reduced rates, short-term traders pay ordinary rates on both, and tax-exempt and tax-deferred accounts pay neither, so each of these groups should be close to indifferent and push the ratio toward 1.0. Investors who do prefer gains to dividends include foreign holders, whose U.S. dividends are subject to withholding while their U.S. capital gains generally are not; at a 15% treaty withholding rate, such an investor values a dividend at about 85 cents on the dollar, close to the estimate here. Taxes therefore remain a candidate explanation, but only through investors outside the domestic individual clientele the original paper had in mind. The yield gradient fits a clientele story, since income-oriented and tax-advantaged holders concentrate in high-yield stocks, but the same pattern also follows from the microstructure explanation below.

5.2 Market Microstructure

Frank and Jagannathan (1998) showed that bid-ask bounce alone can produce a shortfall: if cum-day trades cluster at the bid and ex-day trades at the ask, the measured drop is reduced by up to a full spread. Because the spread is roughly fixed in cents while dividends vary, that effect is proportionally largest for small dividends, which matches the yield gradient in Table 4. Graham, Michaely, and Roberts (2003), on the other hand, found the drop ratio fell as tick sizes shrank, which argues against microstructure as the main force. Our test on nominal prices (Table 7) finds no sign of a tick-size effect. Separating the bid-ask explanation from the tax explanation would require quote data, which this study does not use.

The raw ratios sit in the range of the earlier U.S. literature. The comparison is loose, because the earlier figures are mostly unadjusted close-to-close measures; this study's own close-to-close median, 0.810, is the closest like-for-like figure to Graham et al.'s 0.750.

5.4 Limitations

Several limitations remain. The sample is point-in-time on entry but not on exit: firms that left the S&P 500 before November 2026 are missing. Those firms include acquired, distressed, and demoted companies, whose ex-day behavior may differ, so the estimates describe companies that remained in the index; a database with delisted securities, such as CRSP, would close this gap. Yahoo's opening prints are not audited; the flat-open check in Table 3 suggests the affected events do not change the result. SPY's opening print reflects the market at 9:30 a.m., while a stock's own opening trade can come a little later, and betas are estimated from close-to-close returns but applied to an overnight return. The ±20 trim removes 101 events from the main measure, and the untrimmed result points the same way. Sector labels are current GICS classifications. Finally, the capture test ignores trading costs and taxes, and no test here uses quote data, so the competing explanations in Sections 5.1 and 5.2 cannot be told apart.

SECTION VI

6. Implications

6.1 For Estimating the Ex-Day Price

On average, a stock in this sample opened 0.866 times its dividend below the prior close, after allowing for the market's overnight move, and the figure ran from about 0.80 for low-yield stocks to 0.93 for high-yield stocks. For any single event the noise is far larger than the effect, so these are averages, not forecasts of a given stock's open.

6.2 For Dividend Capture

The shortfall leaves a small, statistically reliable overnight margin of about 6.32 basis points per event net of the market. Spreads, commissions, and the ordinary-income treatment of briefly held dividends all come out of that margin, and this study does not measure them.

6.3 For Market Efficiency

A drop of about 87% of the dividend is a real departure from the frictionless benchmark, but it is the kind of gap that frictions are expected to leave: it is small in dollars, and the trade that would close it carries the costs described above. The results are consistent with an efficient market with frictions and are not evidence of a free arbitrage.

SECTION VII

7. Conclusions

Using 19,319 ex-dividend events from 399 S&P 500 firms over 2010 to 2025, counted only while each firm was in the index, we find that share prices fall by less than the full dividend. Before adjustment the open is lower by 0.777 times the dividend; after adding back each stock's expected overnight market move, the drop is 0.866 times the dividend (95% CI 0.831 to 0.901, clustered by date and firm). The result holds without trimming, without suspect opening prints, and when events are weighted by dividend size.

The shortfall is:

  1. Larger for low-yield stocks (0.798) than for high-yield stocks (0.930), a gradient that survives the market adjustment (p = 0.0006).
  2. Present in every sector, with no significant differences between them (p = 0.725).
  3. Present in 15 of 16 years and significant in 11, with no trend (p = 0.320 for differences across years).
  4. Unrelated to nominal share price.
  5. Concentrated, at the stock level, in a group of 32 stocks with significant shortfalls after false discovery rate control, led by AT&T, Cardinal Health, Boeing, and Ventas, with no stock showing a significant excess.

The October 2026 revision of this paper concluded, from the original's own figures, that the shortfall disappeared after a correctly signed market adjustment. Run on the real prices, that conclusion does not hold: the October figure added back the market's full-day return, which includes moves after the open, and using the overnight move instead leaves a clear shortfall of about 13% of the dividend. Foreign investors' dividend withholding and bid-ask bounce are both consistent with its size and its yield gradient; telling them apart would require ownership or quote data.
