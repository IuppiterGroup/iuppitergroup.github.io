The Ex-Dividend Day Price Drop Anomaly

An Empirical Investigation of 80 S&P 500 Stocks (2010–2025)

Author John, CaesarBot & luppiter Group
Published April 2026
Revised October 2026
Category Finance · Market Microstructure
References 16 References

This study examines whether stock prices decline by the full dividend amount on ex-dividend dates, as predicted by classical financial theory under frictionless markets. Using 5,106 ex-dividend events from 80 S&P 500 component stocks across 10 sectors over the period 2010–2025, we find that the raw close-to-open price drop averages 0.812 of the dividend (bootstrap 95% CI 0.762 to 0.861), below the theoretical value of 1.0 under conventional tests that treat each event as independent (t = −7.35). That shortfall does not survive a correctly signed market adjustment. Market returns on ex-dividend dates were positive on average, which lifts the ex-day open and shrinks the measured drop; adding the market term back, rather than subtracting it as the original version of this paper did, raises the mean drop ratio to approximately 1.0, the frictionless benchmark. This corrected figure is derived from the paper's own reported means, and its standard error cannot be recomputed without the underlying data. Raw drop ratios are higher for medium- and high-yield stocks than for low-yield stocks, and dividend yield is weakly correlated with the drop ratio (Pearson r = 0.043), but the market term weighs most heavily on low-yield stocks, so this pattern may partly reflect the uncorrected market move. Because qualified dividends are taxed at the same reduced rates as long-term capital gains, the tax-clientele model of Elton & Gruber (1970) predicts a ratio near 1.0 for most individual investors, which fits the corrected estimate better than the raw one. Sample selection, the market adjustment, and the inference all carry limitations, set out in Section 5.5, that a rerun on the underlying data must address.

KEYWORDS: ex-dividend day, price drop ratio, tax clientele effect, dividend policy, market microstructure, anomaly

SECTION I

1. Introduction

In a frictionless capital market with no taxes and no transaction costs, a stock's price should decline by exactly the amount of the dividend on the ex-dividend date. This prediction follows directly from the absence of arbitrage: if the stock dropped by less than the dividend, an investor could buy shares the day before, capture the dividend, and sell at a profit; if it dropped by more, an investor could short before and cover after. The Miller & Modigliani (1961) dividend irrelevance proposition provides the theoretical foundation — in perfect markets, dividend policy is irrelevant to firm value, and the ex-day price adjustment should be dollar-for-dollar.

Yet real markets are not frictionless. At least three classes of friction may cause the observed price drop to deviate from the dividend amount:

  1. Differential taxation. In most jurisdictions, dividends and capital gains are taxed at different rates. If the marginal investor faces a higher tax rate on dividends than on capital gains, the equilibrium ex-day price drop will be less than the full dividend, reflecting the after-tax indifference condition. Elton & Gruber (1970) formalized this insight and used the observed drop ratio to infer the marginal tax rate of stockholders.
  2. Transaction costs and market microstructure. Bid-ask spreads, tick sizes, and order-flow dynamics can distort the observed price change. Frank & Jagannathan (1998) showed that even in Hong Kong, where dividends were tax-free, stock prices dropped by less than the dividend, implicating microstructure effects rather than taxes alone. Graham, Michaely, & Roberts (2003), however, found that as U.S. tick sizes shrank from eighths to sixteenths and then to decimals in 2001, ex-day abnormal returns rose and the median drop ratio fell, which they judged inconsistent with microstructure explanations.
  3. Heterogeneous investors and dividend capture. Institutional investors, short-term traders, and tax-exempt or tax-deferred investors (pension funds and retirement accounts) may have different valuations of dividends versus capital gains. Trading around ex-dates ("dividend capture" strategies) introduces additional price dynamics (Lakonishok & Vermaelen, 1986; Michaely & Vila, 1995).

This paper contributes an updated empirical analysis using 15 years of modern market data (2010–2025), spanning the post-decimalization era and encompassing diverse market conditions including the COVID-19 crash, the 2022 bear market, and the AI-driven bull market. We employ parametric and nonparametric tests, bootstrap inference, cross-sectional regressions, and market-adjusted measures to provide a comprehensive picture of the ex-dividend day phenomenon.

SECTION II

2. Literature Review

The study of ex-dividend day price behavior dates to Campbell & Beranek (1955), who first documented that stocks tend to drop by less than the dividend amount. The seminal contribution of Elton & Gruber (1970) formalized the tax-clientele explanation: if the marginal investor's tax rate on dividends exceeds that on capital gains, the equilibrium price drop satisfies:

ΔP / D = (1 − τd) / (1 − τg)

where τd and τg are the tax rates on dividend income and capital gains, respectively. They used observed drop ratios to back out implied marginal tax rates and found evidence consistent with the clientele effect: high-yield stocks (held by lower-tax-bracket investors) exhibited drop ratios closer to 1.0.

Black & Scholes (1974) approached the question from the return side. They concluded that, using the best available empirical methods, it is not possible to show that expected returns on high-yield stocks differ from those on low-yield stocks, either before or after taxes, and therefore that the effect of a change in dividend policy on a firm's stock price cannot be determined from the data.

Kalay (1982) challenged the pure tax interpretation, arguing that transaction costs create a band of no-arbitrage around the theoretical drop, making it impossible to precisely identify the marginal investor's tax rate from price data alone.

Lakonishok & Vermaelen (1986) documented abnormal trading volume around ex-dividend dates, consistent with tax-motivated trading and dividend capture strategies. They found that short-term traders systematically trade around ex-dates to exploit differential tax treatment.

Boyd & Jagannathan (1994) proposed a dynamic model where the drop ratio depends on the relative proportions of long-term investors and short-term dividend capturers in the market.

Bali & Hite (1998) argued that discrete tick sizes (1/8ths in the pre-decimalization era) mechanically prevented prices from adjusting by the exact dividend amount, since dividends rarely fell on tick boundaries. They predicted — and found — that the bias was related to the dividend's position within the tick.

Frank & Jagannathan (1998) provided a critical natural experiment: Hong Kong, where dividends were not taxed. Even there, stock prices dropped by only about 50% of the dividend amount. This evidence strongly supports non-tax explanations, particularly market microstructure effects. They proposed a bid-ask explanation: because market makers have a cost advantage in collecting dividends, they tend to buy cum-dividend at the bid and sell after the ex-date at the ask, so cum-day trades cluster at the bid and ex-day trades at the ask, which mechanically reduces the observed drop.

Graham, Michaely, & Roberts (2003) exploited the NYSE's move from eighths to sixteenths and then to decimal pricing, completed in January 2001. The median drop ratio fell from 0.893 in the eighths era to 0.833 in the sixteenths era and 0.750 in the decimal era, and ex-day abnormal returns rose, the opposite of what tick-size explanations predict. They concluded that their results are inconsistent with microstructure explanations and consistent with the tax explanation, which attributes the shortfall to the preferential taxation of capital gains relative to dividend income.

Elton, Gruber, & Blake (2005) compared taxable and non-taxable closed-end funds, finding that the tax status of investors significantly affects the ex-day price drop, providing clean support for the tax-clientele hypothesis.

Cloyd, Li, & Weaver (2006) examined the interaction of tick sizes and tax effects, finding that both independently contribute to the anomaly.

Zhang, Farrell, & Brown (2008) studied the 2003 dividend tax cut (which reduced the top rate on qualified dividends to 15%) and found that the drop ratio increased after the cut, consistent with tax-based explanations.

Rantapuska (2008), using trading data on all investors in the Finnish stock market, found that investors who prefer dividend income buy cum-dividend and sell ex-dividend while investors with the opposite preference do the reverse, and that transaction costs and dividend yield jointly determine whether any short-term ex-day trading occurs.

SECTION III

3. Data and Methodology

3.1 Sample Construction

We select 80 stocks from the S&P 500, evenly distributed across 10 GICS sectors (8 stocks per sector): Technology, Financials, Healthcare, Energy, Utilities, Consumer Staples, Industrials, Consumer Discretionary, Materials, and Real Estate. All stocks are large-cap and highly liquid. They were selected with hindsight, on the basis of their dividend records, rather than from index membership as it stood at each date, and the paper does not list them; this selection introduces survivorship and look-ahead bias (Section 5.5). Not every stock paid dividends throughout the sample period: the sample has 284 events in 2010 and 298 in 2011, against roughly 320 a year from 2012 on (Table 5). Data are obtained from Yahoo Finance via the yfinance Python library.

For each stock, we retrieve:

  • Complete dividend payment history (2010–2025)
  • Daily OHLCV price data. Yahoo Finance's daily Close is adjusted for stock splits but not for dividends, so the ex-day drop is preserved, but price levels before any later split are back-adjusted rather than the nominal prices that traded at the time. The separately reported Adjusted Close, which also removes dividends, is not used.

The S&P 500 ETF (SPY) is used as a market proxy for beta-adjusted returns.

3.2 Variable Construction

For each ex-dividend event, we compute:

Close-to-Open Drop Ratio:

DRopen = (Closet−1 − Opent) / Dividend

where t is the ex-dividend date and t−1 is the prior trading day.

Close-to-Close Drop Ratio:

DRclose = (Closet−1 − Closet) / Dividend

Annualized Dividend Yield (approximate):

Yield = (4 × Dividend) / Closet−1

This annualization assumes quarterly payment. It understates the yield of monthly payers by a factor of three, which matters for Real Estate: its 619 events exceed the 512 that eight quarterly payers could generate over 16 years, so some of its stocks pay more often than quarterly and are misclassified in the yield terciles (Table 3), the regression (Table 6), and the 40% yield screen (Section 3.3).

Market-Adjusted Drop Ratio:

DRadj = DRopen + (Rm × Closet−1) / Dividend

where Rm is the SPY close-to-close return on the ex-date. A rising market lifts the ex-day open and shrinks the measured drop, so the market term is added back; the original version of this paper subtracted it, which reversed the adjustment. This assumes β = 1, and it applies a full-day market return to a close-to-open drop, a timing mismatch discussed in Section 4.7.

3.3 Filtering

We exclude: (1) observations with drop ratios outside [−20, +20] to remove data errors and extreme outliers; (2) events where annualized yield exceeds 40%, likely representing special dividends or data anomalies. The final sample contains 5,106 ex-dividend events from 80 stocks spanning January 2010 to December 2025. The number of events removed by the ±20 trim is not reported. Because the trim is applied to the outcome variable itself, it can shift the mean, and its effect has not been tested.

3.4 Statistical Tests

  1. One-sample t-test against μ = 1.0 (full dividend drop) and μ = 0.0 (no drop)
  2. Wilcoxon signed-rank test (nonparametric alternative, robust to non-normality)
  3. Bootstrap confidence intervals (10,000 replications, percentile method)
  4. One-way ANOVA for cross-sectional comparisons (by sector, by yield bucket)
  5. Pearson and Spearman correlations between drop ratio and dividend yield
  6. OLS regression of drop ratio on dividend yield
  7. D'Agostino-Pearson normality test on the drop ratio distribution

Each of these tests, including the bootstrap, treats every event as an independent observation. Events that share an ex-date share the same market shock, and events from the same firm share firm characteristics, so the reported standard errors and p-values overstate precision (Petersen, 2009; see Section 5.5).

4.7 Market-Adjusted Results

The original version of this paper subtracted the market term, (Rm × Closet−1) / Dividend, from the raw drop ratio and reported a market-adjusted mean of 0.627 (N = 5,095). That sign was backwards: a rising market lifts the ex-day open and shrinks the measured drop, so the market term must be added. The paper's own figures imply the corrected mean:

Measure N Mean Basis
Raw close-to-open drop ratio 5,106 0.812 Table 1
Mean market term 5,095 ≈ 0.185 0.812 − 0.627, implied by the original subtraction
Market-adjusted, corrected sign 5,095 ≈ 1.00 0.812 + 0.185

The corrected figure is approximate, since it combines means from two samples that differ by 11 events. Its median, standard deviation, standard error, and test against 1.0 cannot be recomputed without the underlying data, because the dispersion of the corrected series depends on the covariance between the raw ratio and the market term, which the paper does not report. The original table's t-statistic for the subtracted series (−8.798) has been removed; it was also inconsistent with its own mean, standard deviation, and N, which imply a t of about −15.2.

Market returns on ex-dividend days were, on average, positive during the sample period, which depressed the raw drop ratio rather than inflating it. Once that effect is added back, the point estimate of the dividend-induced drop is about 1.0, the full dividend. The evidence for a shortfall in this sample therefore does not survive a correctly signed market adjustment. One mismatch remains: the adjustment uses SPY's full-day return, from the prior close to the ex-day close, against a drop measured from the prior close to the ex-day open, so it includes market moves during the ex-day session that cannot affect the opening price. A clean adjustment would use SPY's close-to-open return on the ex-date, ideally with estimated betas rather than β = 1.

SECTION V

5. Discussion

5.1 Tax-Clientele Effects

Our raw close-to-open mean of approximately 0.81 is broadly in line with the earlier literature (Section 5.4). Taken at face value under the Elton-Gruber framework, a drop ratio of 0.81 would imply:

(1 − τd) / (1 − τg) ≈ 0.81

For the current US regime (qualified dividends taxed at 15–20%, long-term capital gains at 15–20%), the implied ratio would be approximately 1.0 for most individual investors. That is higher than the raw ratio but in line with the corrected market-adjusted mean of about 1.0 (Section 4.7), so the raw shortfall does not require a tax explanation. Only if a shortfall were confirmed after a correctly signed and correctly timed market adjustment would it point to either: (a) a marginal investor facing a higher effective dividend tax rate (perhaps due to state taxes, short-term holding periods, or foreign investor withholding), or (b) non-tax factors.

In raw terms, the yield-bucket analysis is consistent with the clientele hypothesis: high-yield stocks (held disproportionately by low-tax or tax-exempt investors) show drop ratios closer to 1.0 (0.878) than low-yield stocks (0.683). The pattern also has a mechanical reading, however. The market term in Section 3.2 scales with Closet−1 / Dividend, the inverse of the per-payment yield, so the positive average market return on ex-dates depresses raw ratios most for low-yield stocks. Until the yield buckets are rerun on correctly adjusted ratios, the pattern cannot be attributed to tax clienteles.

5.2 Market Microstructure

Frank & Jagannathan (1998) demonstrated that microstructure effects alone can explain drop ratios below 1.0 even in the absence of taxes. Two mechanisms are relevant:

  1. Bid-ask bounce. The closing price on the cum-dividend day may occur at the bid, while the opening price on the ex-day may occur at the ask. This mechanical "bounce" reduces the observed price drop by approximately one spread, which can be substantial relative to a small dividend.
  2. Order flow imbalance. Dividend capture trading, buying before and selling after the ex-date, creates excess sell pressure at the ex-date opening. On its own that pressure would push the open lower and enlarge the measured drop, so order flow can explain a shortfall only if the opposite trade dominates: holders who prefer to avoid the dividend selling before the ex-date and buying back after, as Rantapuska (2008) documents for investors with that preference.

Our price-level test (Pearson r = −0.014, p = 0.30) cannot speak to the discrete tick-size explanation (Bali & Hite, 1998), because it runs on split-adjusted prices (Section 4.6). The more direct evidence comes from Graham et al. (2003): the median drop ratio fell, rather than rose, as tick sizes shrank to decimals, which they found inconsistent with microstructure explanations.

5.3 Heterogeneous Investors and Dividend Capture

The substantial cross-sectional and time-series variation in drop ratios is consistent with time-varying composition of the marginal investor population. In years of high volatility (2020, 2022), the increased noise may push estimated drop ratios in either direction. The Utilities sector's high drop ratio (0.93) aligns with its investor base of income-seeking, often tax-advantaged investors.

5.4 Comparison with Prior Literature

Our mean drop ratio of 0.81 (unadjusted) falls within the range reported in the literature:

Study Period Market Drop Ratio
Elton & Gruber (1970) 1966–1967 US ~0.78
Frank & Jagannathan (1998) 1980–1993 Hong Kong ~0.50
Graham et al. (2003) 2001 US (decimal era) 0.75 (median)
This study 2010–2025 US 0.81 (raw mean, close-to-open)

The comparison is loose. Graham et al. (2003) compute the ratio from daily closing prices, which they describe as the standard methodology in prior research, so the benchmarks here should be read as close-to-close measures, while this study's 0.81 is close-to-open; this study's own close-to-close figures (mean 0.862, median 0.912) are the closer like-for-like comparison. Graham et al.'s decimal-era median of 0.750 also follows medians of 0.893 and 0.833 in the eighths and sixteenths eras, and their ratio is not market-adjusted.

5.5 Limitations

Several limitations qualify these results. The 80 stocks are unnamed and were selected with hindsight for dividend histories spanning the sample period, rather than from index membership as it stood at each date, which introduces survivorship and look-ahead bias; the lower event counts in 2010 and 2011 show that the histories were not in fact continuous. The yield measure annualizes each dividend as if it were paid quarterly, which misclassifies monthly payers, evidently including part of the Real Estate sample (Section 3.2). The ±20 trim is applied to the outcome variable, and the number of events it removed is not reported. Every test treats events as independent, although events on the same date share the market shock and events from the same firm share firm characteristics; standard errors that ignore this kind of correlation can be biased (Petersen, 2009), so the very small p-values reported here likely overstate the evidence. The price data are split-adjusted, which invalidates the price-level test, and the paper does not verify that the price and dividend series share the same split basis. Finally, the corrected market-adjusted mean is derived arithmetically from reported means, and the adjustment itself pairs a full-day market return with a close-to-open drop and assumes β = 1.

This revision could not rerun the analysis, because the underlying data were not available to it. Still needed: (1) recompute the market-adjusted ratio with the correct sign, using SPY's close-to-open return on the ex-date and estimated betas, and report its standard error and test against 1.0; (2) rerun the yield, sector, year, and regression results on that corrected ratio; (3) rebuild the sample from point-in-time S&P 500 membership and publish the constituent list; (4) annualize yields from trailing twelve-month dividends; (5) report the number of trimmed events and show results with and without the trim; (6) cluster standard errors by date and by firm; and (7) rerun the price-level test on nominal, unadjusted prices.

SECTION VI

6. Implications

6.1 For Dividend Capture Strategies

A raw drop of 0.81 times the dividend is smaller than the dividend, not larger. Before taxes and trading costs, a naïve "buy at the prior close, sell at the ex-day open" capture trade would therefore have collected the full dividend while losing only 0.81 of it in price, a gain of about 19% of the dividend on average. Once the market return is added back with the correct sign (Section 4.7), the dividend-specific part of that gain is roughly zero, which suggests the raw gain reflects market exposure on ex-dates rather than mispricing. For a taxable U.S. investor, a dividend on shares held this briefly is not a qualified dividend, since that treatment requires holding the shares at least 61 days during the 121-day period that begins 60 days before the ex-dividend date. Bid-ask spreads and commissions also come out of any margin. We have not tested a capture strategy directly, so these results neither establish nor rule out a profitable one.

6.2 For Tax Policy

The historical sensitivity of the drop ratio to tax law changes (Graham et al., 2003; Zhang, Farrell, & Brown, 2008) suggests that dividend taxation can affect stock prices at the margin; the raw yield pattern in this study is weaker evidence, since it may partly reflect the uncorrected market term. Policymakers considering changes to dividend tax rates should expect adjustments in ex-day price behavior and, more broadly, in the relative valuation of dividend-paying versus non-dividend-paying stocks.

6.3 For Market Efficiency

These results do not show a violation of market efficiency. The raw drop ratio below 1.0 is small relative to the noise in individual events, and the correctly signed market adjustment brings the mean to about 1.0, which is what frictionless pricing predicts. Whether any arbitrage profit remains after taxes, transaction costs, and the short-term capital gains treatment of dividend capture trades has not been tested here, so we make no claim either way.

SECTION VII

7. Conclusions

Using 5,106 ex-dividend events from 80 S&P 500 stocks over 2010–2025, we find that the raw close-to-open price drop averages 0.812 of the dividend (95% CI: [0.762, 0.861]), below 1.0 under conventional parametric and nonparametric tests that treat events as independent. That shortfall does not survive a correctly signed market adjustment: adding back the market return on the ex-date, which the original version of this paper subtracted, raises the mean to approximately 1.0, although its standard error and test cannot be recomputed without the underlying data.

In raw terms, the shortfall is:

  • Present in most years but not all: annual means range from 0.517 (2011) to 1.129 (2022) with no secular trend, and 8 of the 16 annual 95% confidence intervals include 1.0
  • Widespread but not uniform across sectors: every sector mean is below 1.0, but Industrials and Consumer Discretionary are not significantly below it, and the sector ANOVA finds no significant differences (p = 0.12)
  • Smaller for higher-yield stocks (ANOVA p = 0.002), a pattern that may partly reflect the uncorrected market term
  • Significant under t-tests, Wilcoxon tests, and bootstrap inference, none of which accounts for clustering by date or firm

The raw shortfall is in line with earlier studies, and differential taxation (Elton & Gruber, 1970), market microstructure frictions (Frank & Jagannathan, 1998), and heterogeneous investor clienteles remain candidate explanations for it. On this paper's own figures, however, the shortfall appears to reflect positive market returns on ex-dates, and a rerun with a correctly signed and correctly timed market adjustment, clustered standard errors, and a point-in-time sample (Section 5.5) is needed before drawing conclusions about taxes, microstructure, or market efficiency.

Revision note, October 2026. This revision corrects the sign of the market adjustment, which the original version subtracted rather than added; the corrected mean drop ratio is about 1.0, not 0.627, so the claimed shortfall does not survive the adjustment. It also fixes the descriptions of Graham, Michaely, & Roberts (2003) and Frank & Jagannathan (1998), the dividend capture arithmetic, and the price data description; conclusions that went beyond the tables have been softened. Missing figures were removed, and the sample, yield, trimming, and inference limitations are now disclosed in Section 5.5.
